Imagine a business that improves its margins by keeping a capable, conscientious person out of the production process. Its automated system already does the job. Adding the worker brings some benefit, but wages, coordination, and other costs exceed what that person adds.

For the business, employing the person has become a losing proposition. The judgment belongs to a particular production arrangement. It tells us very little about the worth of the person whose livelihood has disappeared from the budget.

This essay treats that threshold as a hypothetical future. Today's evidence includes substantial gains from people working with AI. A study of 5,172 customer-support workers, published in the Quarterly Journal of Economics in 2025, found that AI assistance increased the number of issues resolved per hour by about 15% on average. It studied one company's assisted workers, and it gives us no basis for converting that improvement into an equivalent number of lost jobs. [Read the study](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjae044).

Now extend the thought experiment. Suppose automated systems become reliable and inexpensive across most tasks people can sell. They handle physical work as well as software, and provide much of the advice, entertainment, and emotional support for which people currently pay. Human involvement still has uses, but it becomes difficult to earn an adequate living from the work that remains.

Industrialization moved many workers into factories; the expansion of services created other destinations. That history offers reasons for optimism, but it also contains a condition: the expanding sectors continued to buy large amounts of human labor.

A sufficiently capable automation system could weaken that connection. New industries would still appear. Demand could expand as prices fell. Yet the new businesses might need relatively few employees. An economy could create an extraordinary range of products while offering fewer opportunities to earn the money needed to buy them.

The balance matters. If a service becomes cheap enough that demand triples while each unit needs half as much human work, total labor demand can rise. If demand barely grows, the same efficiency gain reduces the amount of work available. The arithmetic does not settle which outcome will dominate. It does explain why both guaranteed mass unemployment and guaranteed replacement jobs are premature conclusions.

During the transition, businesses would probably change unevenly. A software service could automate a process through an update. A factory would need equipment, maintenance, and a workable production schedule. Healthcare providers would need evidence, permission, and a way to pay for deployment. In its discussion of humanoid robots, the International Federation of Robotics identifies practical requirements including cycle times, energy consumption, maintenance, safety, and consistent performance. Intelligence alone does not satisfy that list. [IFR's assessment](https://ifr.org/ifr-press-releases/news/top-5-global-robotics-trends-2026).

These delays could stretch the transition without making it comfortable. A company can stop replacing departing staff before it automates a whole occupation. A graduate can lose an entry route while the profession still looks healthy from a distance. Cheap digital services might arrive years before housing, energy, and physical care become equally affordable. Lower earnings and cheaper entertainment could coexist.

I find that uneven period more plausible than a single date on which work ends. But the eventual question remains: if production needs much less of our labor, what should organize the part of life that work used to occupy?

One possibility is a consumption industry. The phrase needs a careful definition. Consumption has always been an economic activity, and tourism, entertainment, and education already organize experiences. What would change is their place in a society where many people's incomes no longer depend primarily on producing something for an employer.

Such an industry would help people use automated abundance deliberately. A person might commission a course that follows a lifelong curiosity, join an elaborate cooperative game, restore a landscape with robotic assistance, or work with others on a scientific project. Organizations could provide the facilities and coordination, purchase automated services, and make participation accessible. Their customers would pay for experiences and opportunities they wanted to pursue.

Consider a hypothetical operator that helps a group spend a season building and sailing a small boat. It assembles automated design and fabrication services, arranges instruction and access to a workshop, and coordinates the trip. Members choose which tasks to do themselves, even when a machine could finish them faster. They pay a fee from income they receive independently, perhaps wages or a share of returns from productive assets. An offer to license records of the experience is a separate transaction they can decline.

The operator sells an organized opportunity to do something the group wants. Automation could make such ambitious, individually chosen projects affordable to more people. Public institutions and cooperatives could provide similar access. Employment at the operator might be modest even if its service improved many people's lives.

The experience has value even when the participant earns nothing from it. A game can matter because playing it is enjoyable. A garden can matter because someone wants to grow it. An economy that needs fewer working hours could make more room for these activities, provided people can afford the time and resources.

Paying people to consume does not, by itself, create the income that supports consumption. A company might pay a participant because the experience produces useful feedback. It might purchase permission to use someone's history or appearance. It might distribute profits to shareholders. A government might transfer income. Those transactions have different buyers, obligations, and sources of funding. Calling all of them consumption would obscure the arrangements that make them possible.

Paid human participation already exists. Prolific, for example, connects researchers and companies with people who evaluate AI outputs, supply preference judgments, and contribute specialist knowledge. The customer purchases an input that it expects to improve a product or a decision. The participant receives compensation for supplying it. [Prolific's AI services](https://www.prolific.com/ai).

A future version could involve much richer contributions. Consider a cooperative whose members authorize limited uses of their experiences, choices, and ways of responding to particular situations. A product developer might buy access to a representative group of users. A model developer might license specialist demonstrations or pay for continuing feedback on unfamiliar situations. The cooperative could negotiate terms and distribute revenue among its members.

It could also manage personalized simulations. A company might want to test an educational product against agents informed by consenting students, or explore how different groups might respond to a proposed service. Human participation would supply evidence about the people the product is meant to serve. The simulations would have to earn their usefulness through validation.

There is research behind this possibility. Park and colleagues built agents using interviews and survey data from 1,052 people and tested their ability to predict selected attitudes and behaviors. The results support some uses of personal information in behavioral simulation. They do not show that a complete person has been copied, or that a distinctive personality produces a breakthrough in general intelligence. [The revised research paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.10109v3).

For a model company, the useful contribution might be a chef's judgments about ingredients, a technician's diagnosis of an unusual failure, or a person's account of why a service feels intrusive. Personality could matter, but so could experience and knowledge that a personality label fails to capture. A business would need evidence that buying this material improves the outcome it cares about. Expert demonstrations may prove to be a market during the transition rather than a permanent source of income. A lasting market would require continued demand for particular people's participation, preferences, or identities, even when synthetic alternatives become cheap.

Continuing income from personal licensing depends on the rights a contributor retains and whether buyers still want the material. The commercial incentive can weaken after a successful purchase. If one set of interviews lets a company predict many later responses, it may have less reason to commission another interview. Other people or synthetic examples may supply adequate substitutes. A person can contribute to a profitable system without retaining much bargaining power over it.

This is where the idea of digital equity becomes more demanding. Training on someone's data does not automatically make that person a shareholder. Data and model weights are different things, and a contribution to training is not an entry in the company's ownership register.

A continuing royalty would need an agreement defining the permitted uses and the basis for payment. An equity arrangement would need an actual grant of ownership. A cooperative could negotiate either, perhaps accepting some compensation in cash and some in a claim on future revenue or assets. It would also have to decide what happens when a member leaves, when a model changes, or when the original contribution can no longer be traced to a particular use.

These choices affect whether a person remains an occasional supplier or acquires a lasting interest in the business. Even ownership brings uncertainty. A company can fail, retain earnings instead of paying dividends, or issue additional shares. A household should not have to depend on the success of one personality model for its entire livelihood.

A market for human experience could also concentrate payments among famous people or rare specialists. Ordinary preferences might be inexpensive to obtain. Compensation for scarce contributions offers little assurance of an adequate income for everyone else.

I would therefore separate a broad claim on automated production from optional income earned through personal participation. The first could come through widely held productive assets, cooperative ownership, public investment funds, or tax-funded services and transfers. The second could reward useful feedback, creative work, and carefully limited personal licenses. A person could earn more by contributing without having to make private life continuously available to a model company.

The financing would need to survive ordinary economic scrutiny. Dividends require distributable earnings. Public spending requires resources and a workable revenue base. Some automation gains would reach people through lower prices, leaving less profit to distribute. Returns would vary, and ownership would need to extend beyond a single successful model. The IMF's work on AI and fiscal policy already treats the distribution of capital income and the adequacy of social protection as consequential policy questions. It does not promise that any particular income scheme can fund itself. [IMF analysis](https://www.imf.org/en/Blogs/Articles/2024/06/17/fiscal-policy-can-help-broaden-the-gains-of-ai-to-humanity).

With sufficient purchasing power, people could sustain the proposed consumption industry as customers and members. Businesses would compete to help them spend their resources well. Useful measures of success could include whether people achieved goals they chose, learned something they wanted to understand, or valued the time they spent. Paying for an experience and volunteering for a study could remain separate decisions.

Without that independence, the same industry could become a demanding employer under another name. People might have to supply intimate records, perform interesting experiences, or remain continuously engaged to qualify for an income. A personality market could pressure participants to become the kinds of people buyers prefer. Permission to refuse would mean little if refusal made basic living unaffordable.

A workable arrangement would make that freedom practical. People would need clear terms for personal simulations, meaningful limits on reuse, and an explanation of what withdrawal could achieve after training. Shared income should be usable beyond one company's platform. Otherwise, the provider of a person's income could also control most of the places where that income can be spent.

Money would still have a role wherever people compete for limited resources. Automated content can become cheap while a particular place to live, a clean environment, computing capacity, and physical materials remain constrained. Society could distribute some of these through public provision or quotas. A transferable credit accepted for many purchases would perform some familiar functions of money, whatever name appeared on the account.

A life with less paid work could include plenty of demanding activity. People could choose difficult projects, care for others, compete, explore, or make things with little commercial demand. Some would still seek paid work. Others would organize their lives around commitments that never belonged on a payroll. The ability to do so would depend on access to real goods and services, not merely on receiving a nominal balance of digital credits.

An automated economy could give people far more choice over their time. It could also concentrate income so tightly that many people spend their lives competing for small payments from the systems that replaced their jobs. Both outcomes fit the premise of powerful technology. The difference lies in ownership, contracts, and political decisions about who may claim the output.

I hope people will have enough freedom to give different answers to the question of where they go next. The person who wants to raise a child, build a boat, study mathematics, or spend an afternoon with friends should not have to demonstrate a competitive advantage over a machine before being able to live. A successful economy of consumption would give that person resources and room to choose, including the freedom to spend time doing nothing that a model company wants to buy.
