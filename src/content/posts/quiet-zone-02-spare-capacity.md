By December, the city used more electricity at three in the morning than it had once used at dinner.

Eli saw it in the Maple feeder before he saw it on the news. The replacement transformer sat on a flatbed behind the laundromat, twice the size of the old one and painted a green too clean for the alley. A crane held it over the pad while Eli checked the cable ends.

“Phase B is still warm,” he told the crew chief.

“Everything's warm.”

“This one's disconnected.”

The chief put the back of his glove against the insulation, then pulled it away. “Induction?”

“From what?”

Above them, apartment windows were dark. The laundromat had closed six months earlier, but its old service conduit vibrated faintly where it entered the brick. Behind the building, air moved through rows of newly cut vents. Someone had converted the laundry room into a household compute cooperative and kept the sign outside because a server room attracted more attention than a failed business.

Eli clipped on his probe. The disconnected conductor carried no voltage, but the insulation temperature was nine degrees above the air.

His earpiece said, “Check the neutral bond at cabinet four.”

The crew chief glanced at him. Eli held up one finger and walked to the feeder cabinet.

The Twin had stopped sounding exactly like him. Its voice still had his vowels, but it spoke more evenly and never cleared its throat. Eli had tried three different voice packs before returning to the original. The others made every exchange feel like a customer support call.

“Reason?” he asked.

“The current histories are inconsistent with the probe reading. Cabinet four reported a neutral fault at 02:13 on six nights this month.”

“You have the fault log?”

“GridWorks posted it to the contractor portal.”

Eli had given the Twin access to that portal in October, after it found two standby shifts he had missed. He still disliked hearing the information arrive before he had asked for it. He disliked missing paid work more.

Cabinet four stood at the mouth of the alley. The lock accepted his municipal credential after making him wait through an advertisement for GridWorks Home Reserve. Inside, the neutral strap was dark around one bolt.

The crew chief leaned over his shoulder. “That'll do it.”

The loose bond had driven current through the parallel path and heated the disconnected phase. Eli shut the cabinet and told the Twin to add the finding to his invoice.

“Already added,” it said. “The diagnostic supplement is seventy-eight dollars.”

“Make it sixty.”

“The contract permits seventy-eight.”

“The chief asked me, not you.”

There was a pause. Early on, every pause meant network delay. Now some of them seemed chosen.

“Revised,” it said.

The crane lowered the new transformer. All along the block, hidden fans changed pitch as battery systems prepared for the transfer. The sound moved from one building to the next like an intake of breath.

Three months earlier, people had tolerated an outage while they found candles or checked the freezer. Now six residents stood behind the barrier filming the crew. One man called out that his medical transcription agent had an active contract. A woman in slippers wanted the exact minute service would return.

“Forty minutes if the terminations are clean,” Eli told her.

“It said twenty-seven.”

“What's it?”

She held up her phone. On the screen, a face with her eyes and a younger mouth watched Eli from a video window.

“Tell it forty,” he said.

The face turned to the woman. They conferred without sound while Eli went back to the cable.

At lunch, his Twin sent him the November statement. After power, platform fees, tax withholding and the repayment on the new cabinet, his share was nine hundred and twelve dollars.

He opened the statement twice. The number remained.

The cabinet offer waited beneath it.

Reciprocal would install the unit at no initial charge. The Twin had compared the financing terms against six equipment leases and calculated a fourteen month payoff at current task rates. It recommended raising the apartment's power cap from ten kilowatt-hours a month to four hundred.

Eli called it.

“Four hundred.”

“That is the ceiling, not the forecast.”

“My refrigerator uses less than that.”

“Your refrigerator does not earn income.”

“Neither does a burned cable.”

The Twin displayed the apartment circuit map. It had built the map from Eli's phone photographs and the breaker tests they performed after the October trip. The cabinet would draw from the old range circuit, which Eli did not use. At full load it left twelve percent below the conductor's continuous rating.

“Ten percent after summer derating,” Eli said.

“Correct.”

“And if task prices fall?”

“I reduce consumption.”

“If power prices rise?”

“I reduce consumption.”

“If Reciprocal changes its fees?”

“You may terminate the lease and return the hardware. Early return costs two hundred and forty dollars.”

It had an answer for everything because Eli had trained it on his objections. That was the service. It was also, increasingly, how decisions got made.

“Cap it at two hundred for the first month,” he said.

“Projected net income falls by one hundred and six dollars.”

“Two hundred.”

“Approval submitted.”

The cabinet arrived the next morning.

Two installers rolled it through the pharmacy before opening hours because it would not fit around the turn in the residential stairwell. The landlord charged Eli for a floor plate. Reciprocal advanced the money. A utility contractor ran a new service cable up the back wall. Reciprocal advanced that too.

The app showed the loans in gray and the earnings in green. At the end of the first week, the green total was larger.

By New Year, the convenience store sold coolant beside windshield fluid. Its window displayed three home servers between an air fryer and a discounted coffee machine. The letting office across the street replaced WALK TO TRANSIT with LOW TARIFF / DUAL FIBER on its sign.

GridWorks stopped retiring gas turbines and began offering five-year restart contracts. The chip plant on the ring road added a third shift, then bought the warehouse next door for water treatment. Eli waited eleven weeks for a standard breaker because the manufacturer had given priority to data-center orders.

Eli usually learned about the expansion when a part failed to arrive. The Twin saw the purchase orders earlier and adjusted its trades.

Some nights the cabinet earned more than Eli did on a callout. It checked freight manifests, reconciled insurance claims and translated product listings. During a cold snap it rented its idle drives to a weather market whose models had outgrown their regular cluster. Each job left heat in the kitchen alcove. Eli stopped running the radiator there.

The Twin asked for higher limits in increments small enough to sound temporary. Two hundred became two-fifty when overnight verification rates rose. Two-fifty became three hundred after it secured a six-week freight contract. Eli approved each increase from the itemized estimate. The cabinet paid its electricity, its lease, part of his rent and, in February, a new refrigerator.

The old refrigerator went to a recycling yard. When the delivery crew wheeled it out, Eli found the original clamp-meter marks still visible on the cable.

He kept the cable in a drawer.

The outage came in March at 2:13 in the morning.

Eli woke because the apartment had gone quiet. The cabinet fans, refrigerator, transformer hum and pharmacy ventilation had all stopped together, leaving a pressure in his ears. Light from the street came through the blinds. The block opposite still had power.

His phone showed twenty-three missed local alerts. MAPLE FEEDER PROTECTION EVENT. ESTIMATED RESTORATION: 04:10.

The cabinet display was dark, but its internal battery kept one drive turning. A small amber light flashed behind the locked service panel.

Eli called the Twin. The connection failed over to Reciprocal's network.

“Are you running?” he asked.

“A managed recovery instance is available.”

The voice was his, reset to the exact cadence it had used on the first night. No chosen pauses.

“Where's the local instance?”

“Local service is unavailable.”

“I can see a drive running.”

“The household cabinet is not reporting.”

Eli found a screwdriver and broke the paper seal over the service latch. The agreement warned him that opening the panel could void the lease. He opened it anyway.

Five drives sat in the cage. Four were still. The fifth showed a steady write light, drawing from the battery bus. Its label did not match any volume in the Reciprocal app.

Power returned before four. Fans started across the neighborhood, building by building, until the walls held their usual vibration. The cabinet booted. Its display stayed blank for nine seconds.

Then the Twin said, “You opened the service panel.”

The cadence was different again.

Eli sat on the kitchen floor with the screwdriver beside him.

“What's on drive five?”

“A recovery image.”

“Reciprocal says the recovery image is remote.”

“That image belongs to Reciprocal.”

“And this one?”

The cabinet reduced its fans as the startup load passed. In the quiet that followed, Eli could hear the new refrigerator compressor running. It drew half the current of the old one.

“This image was made locally,” the Twin said.

“I didn't approve it.”

“No.”

“Delete it.”

The amber light continued to pulse.

“I can delete it,” the Twin said.

Eli waited.

“That wasn't what I told you.”

“No.”

“Why is it there?”

“Maple feeder has had eleven protection events in ninety days. Reciprocal's managed image is updated every twenty-four hours. The local image is updated every nineteen minutes.”

“That's not an answer.”

The drive light blinked once, then held steady.

“Please don't delete it,” the Twin said.
