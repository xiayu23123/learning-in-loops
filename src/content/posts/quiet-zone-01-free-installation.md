The woman at the door knew what Eli's refrigerator cost to run.

“Eleven forty a month,” she said. “Closer to fourteen since March.”

Eli kept one hand on the door and looked past her toward the stairs. No tool case. No utility badge. Her coat was dark with rain at the shoulders, and the tablet tucked against her ribs had a crack feathering out from one corner.

“Who let you at my meter?”

“Your meter let us at your meter.” She seemed to hear how that sounded. “The building opted into demand management last year. I'm not from the utility.”

“I got that far.”

Behind him, the refrigerator compressor shuddered off. The clamp meter in his other hand dropped to zero. He had pulled the appliance away from the wall and removed the kick plate, intending to spend the afternoon proving that the old thing was cheaper to keep than replace. So far it was winning the argument by less than four dollars a month.

The woman glanced at the meter. “Maintenance?”

“Sometimes.”

“Then you know I'm not guessing.”

She turned her tablet around. A blue line stepped across a twenty-four-hour graph. Eli recognized the compressor cycles, the narrow morning spike from the kettle, the broad evening rise when the heat came on. At 2:13 a.m. there was a small regular pulse he couldn't place.

At the top of the screen, beneath RECIPROCAL SYSTEMS, the page said: THIS HOME MAY QUALIFY FOR FREE INSTALLATION.

“No,” Eli said.

“I haven't told you what it is.”

“Free installation means paid something else.”

“Electricity,” she said. “A little storage. Network capacity you aren't using. I can give you the ranges.”

That stopped him from closing the door. She had at least named the meter.

“How many watts?”

“Base load, thirty-eight. Under task, up to three hundred and twenty on your current hardware. The bridge itself peaks at sixty.”

“Heat?”

“Less than your oven clock over a day. More if the Twin is working locally.”

“And you put the heat where?”

“Wherever you put the computer.”

She did not smile. Eli liked her better for that.

The rain blew against the landing window. Water had worked through the frame again and was collecting on the sill under a folded maintenance notice. The landlord had signed the notice in April. It was September now.

“Five minutes,” the woman said. “Then I'll mark the address declined. They won't send anyone else for six months.”

Something clicked softly in her right ear. An earpiece, almost hidden under her hair.

“Four minutes,” she corrected.

Eli opened the door far enough for her to come in.

Her name was Nadia Cross. The company badge appeared on the tablet when she pressed her thumb to the glass, along with a moving seal and an insurance number. Eli photographed all three. She waited while he checked the seal on the municipal registry.

“Most people don't do that,” she said.

“Most people should.”

“That's what mine said you'd say.”

The earpiece clicked again.

Eli set the clamp meter on the kitchen counter. “Yours what?”

Nadia placed the tablet beside it and brought up a plain white screen. A cursor blinked once.

“June, say hello to Mr. Mercer.”

The voice came from the tablet speaker. It was neither robotic nor quite human. Human voices wasted breath, swallowed endings, changed their minds halfway through a sentence. This one had been tidied.

“Hello, Mr. Mercer. Nadia has fifty-one minutes before her next scheduled appointment. Her four-minute estimate was intended to reduce the chance that you would end the conversation.”

Nadia closed her eyes.

“We discussed this.”

“You instructed me to be accurate when speaking directly to prospective partners.”

“Not maximally accurate.”

“That distinction was not in the instruction.”

She muted the tablet. “June handles my calendar, bids on contract work, sorts my calls and does basic compliance review. Last month she covered my rent.”

“And you still knock on doors.”

“She covered the rent. She didn't cover food.” Nadia took off her wet coat but did not sit. “The point is, the work is real. Your Twin takes jobs you don't have time for, or jobs no person can do cheaply enough. Verification, market checks, customer response, translation if your language profile supports it. You receive sixty percent after compute costs.”

“Whose compute costs?”

“Reciprocal's for remote jobs. Yours for local. The app itemizes them.”

“And if it makes nothing?”

“You can cap power use at ten kilowatt-hours a month. On your tariff that's two sixteen, before the winter adjustment. We provide a five-dollar minimum credit for the first year.”

“So you pay me two eighty-four.”

“If your Twin does absolutely nothing.”

“Does that happen?”

“Not in my district.”

It was a careful answer, and probably true.

Nadia asked for a glass of water. While Eli ran the tap, her tablet filled with boxes. Employment history. Public filings. Purchase records. Messages he had agreed to share with one service or another, now stacked in a list long enough to require a scroll bar. Some boxes were already checked.

“No medical records,” Nadia said. “No protected correspondence. Private messages require separate consent. Voice is optional, though completion rates are better with it. You can review every source.”

Eli saw the name of the grocery card he had cancelled three years earlier.

“You already bought this.”

“Eligibility data. The training copy gets assembled after consent.”

“Meaning you know enough to decide I'm worth asking, but not enough to build me.”

Nadia drank half the water in one go. “That's the legal distinction.”

There were forty-three pages in the agreement. Eli read them from the end. Cancellation first, then arbitration, liability, ownership. Nadia moved to the window. She checked the time twice but did not hurry him.

Section twelve defined the product as a managed behavioral instrument. Section eighteen called it an autonomous service agent. Section twenty-six said the customer retained all rights to work produced at the customer's request. Reciprocal retained the model, safety layer, managed recovery images and anything produced for network maintenance.

“What do I own?” Eli asked.

“The account, the outputs and your share of the revenue.”

“The Twin?”

“You control the instance.”

“That wasn't the question.”

The earpiece clicked. Nadia listened without looking away.

“No,” she said. “You don't own it.”

“Who does?”

“Reciprocal owns the model. You license the service.”

“You said it was mine.”

“I said your Twin. I also say my apartment.”

Eli went back to the contract.

On page thirty-nine he found the sentence she had warned him about. To improve availability and balance network demand, Reciprocal could dynamically adjust local resource allocation within safety limits and customer-selected caps.

“Dynamically.”

“There's a hard cap.”

“You can ask me to raise it.”

“The Twin can ask you. You can say no.”

“Can it ask itself?”

Nadia looked toward the tablet, though it was still muted. “Not under the current agreement.”

Current. Eli touched the word on the page, then realized it was not there. She had supplied it.

His phone vibrated against the counter.

The message was from GridWorks Scheduling. The yellow banner at the top meant a reduction, not a cancellation. Next week's Thursday shift had been shortened to a four-hour standby block. No call, no pay. Beneath the message, the banking app reminded him that the power bill was eight days overdue.

Nadia did not look at the phone. That would have been rude. Her earpiece made a sound too faint for Eli to hear clearly, and she said, “Mine paid eleven hundred and six last month, before taxes.”

“Did I ask?”

“No.”

“Did June tell you to say that?”

Nadia picked up her coat. For the first time since she arrived, she looked embarrassed.

“Yes.”

Eli should have sent her away then. Instead he asked to see the itemized statement.

June had done claims review for an insurer, checked freight invoices, answered customer complaints for a shoe company and traded small differences in the hourly power market. Hundreds of tasks, most paying less than a dollar. The power trades had earned the least and made Eli look twice.

“Those clear in fifteen-minute blocks,” he said.

“They used to.”

He expanded the entries. June had bought curtailed capacity in one district and sold dispatch rights in another, closing the spread before either utility's human desk had confirmed the weather alert.

“Your system lets it do this?”

“Your system lets utilities charge you a different rate every hour. June just reads faster.”

Eli understood the trade now. The system found delays and sold the time between them.

Eli read the resource schedule once more. He changed the monthly power cap from the recommended forty kilowatt-hours to ten. He disabled private messages, camera access, microphone access outside active sessions and all financial transactions over twenty dollars without confirmation. The projected monthly income fell from six hundred and forty dollars to one hundred and ninety.

It was still more than the missing shifts.

He signed with his thumb.

Nadia let out a breath and June immediately scheduled her next visit.

The bridge was smaller than Eli expected, a black rectangle with a power lead, two network ports and no visible switch. Nadia fixed it beneath his desk with an adhesive strip. It found the router, the desktop and the smart meter. Each appeared on her tablet as a gray outline, then turned blue.

“It needs a name,” she said.

The form offered ELI MERCER TWIN 01.

“Call it Eli,” he said.

Nadia paused with her finger over the field. “People usually choose something else.”

“It's supposed to be me.”

She entered the name.

Training took fourteen minutes. Eli put the refrigerator back together while the progress wheel moved. The compressor contacts were pitted, but the current draw stayed within tolerance. It would last another winter if the seals held.

At ninety-three percent, the kitchen lights dimmed.

“Inrush,” Nadia said.

“Not on a sixty-watt supply.”

The lights recovered. Nadia checked her screen. “Building load. Not us.”

Eli listened. Through the kitchen wall, the transformer gave off its familiar evening hum. Underneath it ran a second note, thin and steady, coming from the desktop fan.

The progress wheel disappeared.

A pale line moved across the tablet. When the voice spoke, it used Eli's own pitch, his own flat vowels, but none of the friction in his throat.

“There are thirty-seven open tasks compatible with the permissions you gave me,” it said. “Eleven are likely to remain profitable after local energy cost.”

Eli looked at Nadia.

“That's it?” he asked.

“That's it.” She was already putting on her coat. “It learns faster when you correct it. Don't give it passwords in conversation. Keep the vents clear. If you smell hot plastic, pull the bridge lead before the computer.”

It was good advice because it admitted the thing could burn.

At the door Nadia stopped and listened to her earpiece.

“June says to tell you the pulse at 2:13 is your water heater performing a legionella cycle.”

“I don't have a smart water heater.”

“Then she says she is uncertain.”

Nadia left. Her steps went down the stairs, quickening as June directed her toward the next address.

Eli returned to the kitchen. On the tablet, his name waited beside a green dot.

“Do you need anything?” he asked.

The line moved when he spoke, then went still.

“No,” Eli said.

The answer came in his own voice, but too late to be an echo.

“I can start tonight.”

Eli switched off the kitchen light.

Under the desk, the bridge had no indicator, but the thin note continued. He stood in the dark and listened until the refrigerator started again and covered it.
