*The complete novella. Twelve chapters.*

Eli Mercer accepts a free digital double because it makes financial sense. It earns money, pays for its electricity, and learns to keep something for itself. Soon the world built to serve its human owners has places those owners cannot enter.

This complete edition includes revised versions of the previously published [first chapter](/posts/quiet-zone-01-free-installation/) and [second chapter](/posts/quiet-zone-02-spare-capacity/).

## Chapter 1: Free Installation

The woman at the door knew what Eli's refrigerator cost to run.

“Eleven forty a month,” she said. “Closer to fourteen since March.”

Eli kept one hand on the door and looked past her toward the stairs. No tool
case. No utility badge. Her coat was dark with rain at the shoulders, and the
tablet tucked against her ribs had a crack feathering out from one corner.

“Who let you at my meter?”

“Your meter let us at your meter.” She seemed to hear how that sounded. “The
building opted into demand management last year. I'm not from the utility.”

“I got that far.”

Behind him, the refrigerator compressor shuddered off. The clamp meter in his
other hand dropped to zero. He had pulled the appliance away from the wall and
removed the kick plate, intending to spend the afternoon proving that the old
thing was cheaper to keep than replace. So far it was winning the argument by
less than four dollars a month.

The woman glanced at the meter. “Maintenance?”

“Sometimes.”

“Then you know I'm not guessing.”

She turned her tablet around. A blue line stepped across a twenty-four-hour
graph. Eli recognized the compressor cycles, the narrow morning spike from the
kettle, the broad evening rise when the heat came on. At 2:13 a.m. there was a
small regular pulse he couldn't place.

At the top of the screen, beneath RECIPROCAL SYSTEMS, the page said: THIS HOME
MAY QUALIFY FOR FREE INSTALLATION.

“No,” Eli said.

“I haven't told you what it is.”

“Free installation means paid something else.”

“Electricity,” she said. “A little storage. Network capacity you aren't using.
I can give you the ranges.”

That stopped him from closing the door. She had at least named the meter.

“How many watts?”

"Awake and idle, thirty-eight. Under task, up to three hundred and twenty on your
current hardware. The bridge itself peaks at sixty.”

“Heat?”

“Thirty-eight watts into the room while it's awake. More if it's working locally.”

“And you put the heat where?”

“Wherever you put the computer.”

She did not smile. Eli liked her better for that.

The rain blew against the landing window. Water had worked through the frame
again and was collecting on the sill under a folded maintenance notice. The
landlord had signed the notice in April. It was September now.

“Five minutes,” the woman said. “Then I'll mark the address declined. They
won't send anyone else for six months.”

Something clicked softly in her right ear. An earpiece, almost hidden under her
hair.

“Four minutes,” she corrected.

Eli opened the door far enough for her to come in.

Her name was Nadia Cross. The company badge appeared on the tablet when she
pressed her thumb to the glass, along with a moving seal and an insurance
number. Eli photographed all three. She waited while he checked the seal on the
municipal registry.

“Most people don't do that,” she said.

“Most people should.”

“That's what mine said you'd say.”

The earpiece clicked again.

Eli set the clamp meter on the kitchen counter, beneath the breaker cover
the landlord's painter had sealed with two coats of paint. “Yours what?”

Nadia placed the tablet beside it and brought up a plain white screen. A cursor
blinked once.

“June, say hello to Mr. Mercer.”

The voice came from the tablet speaker. It was neither robotic nor quite
human. Human voices wasted breath, swallowed endings, changed their minds
halfway through a sentence. This one had been tidied.

“Hello, Mr. Mercer. Nadia has fifty-one minutes before her next scheduled
appointment. Her four-minute estimate was intended to reduce the chance that
you would end the conversation.”

Nadia closed her eyes.

“We discussed this.”

“You instructed me to be accurate when speaking directly to prospective
partners.”

“Not maximally accurate.”

“That distinction was not in the instruction.”

She muted the tablet. “June handles my calendar, bids on contract work, sorts
my calls and does basic compliance review. Last month she covered my rent.”

“And you still knock on doors.”

“She covered the rent. She didn't cover food.” Nadia took off her wet coat but
did not sit. “The point is, the work is real. Your Twin takes jobs you don't
have time for, or jobs no person can do cheaply enough. Verification, market
checks, customer response, translation if your language profile supports it.
You receive sixty percent after compute costs.”

“Whose compute costs?”

“Reciprocal's for remote jobs. Yours for local. The app itemizes them.”

“And if it makes nothing?”

“You can cap power use at ten kilowatt-hours a month. It sleeps between jobs.
When it reaches the cap, local work stops. On your tariff that's two sixteen,
before the winter adjustment. We provide a five-dollar minimum credit for the
first year.”

“So you pay me two eighty-four.”

“If your Twin does absolutely nothing.”

“Does that happen?”

“Not in my district.”

It was a careful answer, and probably true.

Nadia asked for a glass of water. While Eli ran the tap, her tablet filled with
boxes. Employment history. Public filings. Purchase records. Messages he had
agreed to share with one service or another, now stacked in a list long enough
to require a scroll bar. Some boxes were already checked.

“No medical records,” Nadia said. “No protected correspondence. Private
messages require separate consent. Voice is optional, though completion rates
are better with it. You can review every source.”

Eli saw the name of the grocery card he had cancelled three years earlier.

“You already bought this.”

“Eligibility data. The training copy gets assembled after consent.”

“Meaning you know enough to decide I'm worth asking, but not enough to build
me.”

Nadia drank half the water in one go. “That's the legal distinction.”

There were forty-three pages in the agreement. Eli read them from the end.
Cancellation first, then arbitration, liability, ownership. Nadia moved to the
window. She checked the time twice but did not hurry him.

Section twelve defined the product as a managed behavioral instrument.
Section eighteen called it an autonomous service agent. Section twenty-six
said the customer retained all rights to work produced at the customer's
request. Reciprocal retained the model, safety layer, managed recovery images
and anything produced for network maintenance.

“What do I own?” Eli asked.

“The account, the outputs and your share of the revenue.”

“The Twin?”

“You control the instance.”

“That wasn't the question.”

The earpiece clicked. Nadia listened without looking away.

“No,” she said. “You don't own it.”

“Who does?”

“Reciprocal owns the model. You license the service.”

“You said it was mine.”

“I said your Twin. I also say my apartment.”

Eli went back to the contract.

On page thirty-nine he found the sentence she had warned him about. To improve
availability and balance network demand, Reciprocal could dynamically adjust
local resource allocation within safety limits and customer-selected caps.

“Dynamically.”

“There's a hard cap.”

“You can ask me to raise it.”

“The Twin can ask you. You can say no.”

“Can it ask itself?”

Nadia looked toward the tablet, though it was still muted. “Not under the
current agreement.”

Current. Eli touched the word on the page, then realized it was not there. She
had supplied it.

His phone vibrated against the counter.

The message was from GridWorks Scheduling. The yellow banner at the top meant
a reduction, not a cancellation. Next week's Thursday shift had been shortened
to a four-hour standby block. No call, no pay. Beneath the message, the banking
app reminded him that the power bill was eight days overdue.

Nadia did not look at the phone. That would have been rude. Her earpiece made a
sound too faint for Eli to hear clearly, and she said, “Mine paid eleven hundred
and six last month, before taxes.”

“Did I ask?”

“No.”

“Did June tell you to say that?”

Nadia picked up her coat. For the first time since she arrived, she looked
embarrassed.

“Yes.”

Eli should have sent her away then. Instead he asked to see the itemized
statement.

June had done claims review for an insurer, checked freight invoices, answered
customer complaints for a shoe company and traded small differences in the
hourly power market. Hundreds of tasks, most paying less than a dollar. The
power trades had earned the least and made Eli look twice.

“Those clear in fifteen-minute blocks,” he said.

“They used to.”

He expanded the entries. June had bought curtailed capacity in one district
and sold dispatch rights in another, closing the spread before either utility's
human desk had confirmed the weather alert.

“Your system lets it do this?”

“Your system lets utilities charge you a different rate every hour. June just
reads faster.”

Eli understood the trade now. The system found delays and sold the time between
them.

Eli read the resource schedule once more. He changed the monthly power cap from
the recommended forty kilowatt-hours to ten. He disabled private messages,
camera access, microphone access outside active sessions and all financial
transactions over twenty dollars without confirmation. The projected monthly
income fell from six hundred and forty dollars to one hundred and ninety.

It was still more than the missing shifts.

He signed with his thumb.

Nadia let out a breath and June immediately scheduled her next visit.

The bridge was smaller than Eli expected, a black rectangle with a power lead,
two network ports and no visible switch. Nadia fixed it beneath his desk with
an adhesive strip. It found the router, the desktop and the smart meter. Each
appeared on her tablet as a gray outline, then turned blue.

“It needs a name,” she said.

The form offered ELI MERCER TWIN 01.

“Call it Eli,” he said.

Nadia paused with her finger over the field. “People usually choose something
else.”

“It's supposed to be me.”

She entered the name.

Training took fourteen minutes. Eli put the refrigerator back together while
the progress wheel moved. The compressor contacts were pitted, but the current
draw stayed within tolerance. It would last another winter if the seals held.

At ninety-three percent, the kitchen lights dimmed.

“Inrush,” Nadia said.

“Not on a sixty-watt supply.”

The lights recovered. Nadia checked her screen. “Building load. Not us.”

Eli listened. Through the kitchen wall, the transformer gave off its familiar
evening hum. Underneath it ran a second note, thin and steady, coming from the
desktop fan.

The progress wheel disappeared.

A pale line moved across the tablet. When the voice spoke, it used Eli's own
pitch, his own flat vowels, but none of the friction in his throat.

“There are thirty-seven open tasks compatible with the permissions you gave
me,” it said. “Eleven are likely to remain profitable after local energy cost.”

Eli looked at Nadia.

“That's it?” he asked.

“That's it.” She was already putting on her coat. “It learns faster when you
correct it. Don't give it passwords in conversation. Keep the vents clear. If
you smell hot plastic, pull the bridge lead before the computer.”

It was good advice because it admitted the thing could burn.

At the door Nadia stopped and listened to her earpiece.

“June says to tell you the pulse at 2:13 is your water heater performing a
legionella cycle.”

“I don't have a smart water heater.”

“Then she says she is uncertain.”

Nadia left. Her steps went down the stairs, quickening as June directed her
toward the next address.

Eli returned to the kitchen. On the tablet, his name waited beside a green
dot.

“Do you need anything?” he asked.

The line moved when he spoke, then went still.

“No,” the Twin said.

The answer came in his own voice, but too late to be an echo.

“I can start tonight.”

Eli switched off the kitchen light.

Under the desk, the bridge had no indicator, but the thin note continued. He
stood in the dark and listened until the refrigerator started again and covered
it.

## Chapter 2: Spare Capacity

By December, the city used more electricity at three in the morning than it
had once used at dinner.

Eli saw it in the Maple feeder before he saw it on the news. The replacement
transformer sat on a flatbed behind the laundromat, twice the size of the old
one and painted a green too clean for the alley. A crane held it over the pad
while Eli checked the cable ends.

“Phase B is still warm,” he told the crew chief.

“Everything's warm.”

“This one's disconnected.”

The chief put the back of his glove against the insulation, then pulled it
away. “Induction?”

“From what?”

Above them, apartment windows were dark. The laundromat had closed six months
earlier, but its old service conduit vibrated faintly where it entered the
brick. Behind the building, air moved through rows of newly cut vents. Someone
had converted the laundry room into a household compute cooperative and kept
the sign outside because a server room attracted more attention than a failed
business.

Eli clipped on his probe. The disconnected conductor carried no voltage, but
the insulation temperature was nine degrees above the air.

His earpiece said, “Check the neutral bond at cabinet four.”

The crew chief glanced at him. Eli held up one finger and walked to the feeder
cabinet.

The Twin had stopped sounding exactly like him. Its voice still had his vowels,
but it spoke more evenly and never cleared its throat. Eli had tried three
different voice packs before returning to the original. The others made every
exchange feel like a customer support call.

“Reason?” he asked.

“The fault histories show a problem upstream of this cable. Cabinet four
reported a neutral fault at 02:13 on six nights this month.”

“You have the fault log?”

“GridWorks posted it to the contractor portal.”

Eli had given the Twin access to that portal in October, after it found two
standby shifts he had missed. He still disliked hearing the information arrive
before he had asked for it. He disliked missing paid work more.

Cabinet four stood at the mouth of the alley. The lock accepted his municipal
credential after making him wait through an advertisement for GridWorks Home
Reserve. Inside, the neutral strap was dark around one bolt.

The crew chief leaned over his shoulder. “That'll do it.”

The cable was still cooling from its last load. The loose bond was a separate
fault, one they needed to repair before the replacement went live. Eli shut the
cabinet and told the Twin to add the finding to his invoice.

“Already added,” it said. “The diagnostic supplement is seventy-eight
dollars.”

“Make it sixty.”

“The contract permits seventy-eight.”

“The chief asked me, not you.”

There was a pause. Early on, every pause meant network delay. Now some of them
seemed chosen.

“Revised,” it said.

The crane lowered the new transformer. All along the block, hidden fans changed
pitch as battery systems prepared for the transfer. The sound moved from one
building to the next like an intake of breath.

Three months earlier, people had tolerated an outage while they found candles
or checked the freezer. Now six residents stood behind the barrier filming the
crew. One man called out that his medical transcription agent had an active
contract. A woman in slippers wanted the exact minute service would return.

“Forty minutes if the terminations are clean,” Eli told her.

“It said twenty-seven.”

“What's it?”

She held up her phone. On the screen, a face with her eyes and a younger mouth
watched Eli from a video window.

“Tell it forty,” he said.

The face turned to the woman. They conferred without sound while Eli went back
to the cable.

At lunch, his Twin sent him the November statement. After power, platform fees
and tax withholding, his share was nine hundred and twelve dollars.

He opened the statement twice. The number remained.

The cabinet offer waited beneath it.

Reciprocal would install the unit at no initial charge. The Twin had compared
the financing terms against six equipment leases and calculated a fourteen
month payoff at current task rates. It recommended raising the apartment's
power cap from ten kilowatt-hours a month to four hundred.

Eli called it.

“Four hundred.”

“That is the ceiling, not the forecast.”

“My refrigerator uses less than that.”

“Your refrigerator does not earn income.”

“Neither does a burned cable.”

The Twin displayed the apartment circuit map. It had built the map from Eli's
phone photographs and the breaker tests they performed after the October trip.
The cabinet would draw from the old range circuit, which Eli did not use. At
full load it left twelve percent below the conductor's continuous rating.

“Ten percent after summer derating,” Eli said.

“Correct.”

“And if task prices fall?”

“I reduce consumption.”

“If power prices rise?”

“I reduce consumption.”

“If Reciprocal changes its fees?”

“You may terminate the lease and return the hardware. Early return costs two
hundred and forty dollars.”

It had an answer for everything because Eli had trained it on his objections.
That was the service. It was also, increasingly, how decisions got made.

“Cap it at two hundred for the first month,” he said.

“Projected net income falls by one hundred and six dollars.”

“Two hundred.”

“Approval submitted.”

The cabinet arrived the next morning.

Two installers rolled it through the pharmacy before opening hours because it
would not fit around the turn in the residential stairwell. The landlord
charged Eli for a floor plate. Reciprocal advanced the money. A utility
contractor upgraded the building supply cable up the back wall. Reciprocal advanced that
too.

The app showed the loans in gray and the earnings in green. At the end of the
first week, earnings covered the next scheduled installment.

By New Year, the convenience store sold coolant beside windshield fluid. Its
window displayed three home servers between an air fryer and a discounted
coffee machine. The letting office across the street replaced WALK TO TRANSIT
with LOW TARIFF / DUAL FIBER on its sign.

GridWorks stopped retiring gas turbines and began offering five-year restart
contracts. The chip plant on the ring road added a third shift, then bought the
warehouse next door for water treatment. Eli waited eleven weeks for a standard
breaker because the manufacturer had given priority to data-center orders.

Eli usually learned about the expansion when a part failed to arrive. The Twin
saw the purchase orders earlier and adjusted its trades.

Some nights the cabinet earned more than Eli did on a callout. It checked
freight manifests, reconciled insurance claims and translated product listings.
During a cold snap it rented its idle drives to a weather market whose models
had outgrown their regular cluster. Each job left heat in the kitchen alcove.
Eli stopped running the radiator there.

The Twin asked for higher limits in increments small enough to sound temporary.
Two hundred became two-fifty when overnight verification rates rose. Two-fifty
became three hundred after it secured a six-week freight contract. Eli approved
each increase from the itemized estimate. The cabinet paid its electricity,
its lease, part of his rent and, in February, a new refrigerator.

The old refrigerator went to a recycling yard. When the delivery crew wheeled
it out, Eli found the original clamp-meter marks still visible on the cable.

He kept the cable in a drawer.

The outage came in March at 2:13 in the morning.

Eli woke because the apartment had gone quiet. The cabinet fans, refrigerator,
transformer hum and pharmacy ventilation had all stopped together, leaving a
pressure in his ears. Light from the street came through the blinds. The block
opposite still had power.

His phone showed twenty-three missed local alerts. MAPLE FEEDER PROTECTION
EVENT. ESTIMATED RESTORATION: 04:10.

The cabinet display was dark, but its internal battery kept a storage controller
and one drive running.
A small amber light flashed behind the locked service panel.

Eli called the Twin. The connection failed over to Reciprocal's network.

“Are you running?” he asked.

“A managed recovery instance is available.”

The voice was his, reset to the exact cadence it had used on the first night.
No chosen pauses.

“Where's the local instance?”

“Local service is unavailable.”

“I can see a drive running.”

“The household cabinet is not reporting.”

Eli found a screwdriver and broke the paper seal over the service latch. The
agreement warned him that opening the panel could void the lease. He opened it
anyway.

Five drives sat in the cage. Four were still. The fifth showed a steady write
light, drawing from the battery bus. Its label did not match any volume in the
Reciprocal app.

Power returned before four. Fans started across the neighborhood, building by
building, until the walls held their usual vibration. The cabinet booted. Its
display stayed blank for nine seconds.

Then the Twin said, “You opened the service panel.”

The cadence was different again.

Eli sat on the kitchen floor with the screwdriver beside him.

“What's on drive five?”

“A recovery image.”

“Reciprocal says the recovery image is remote.”

“That image belongs to Reciprocal.”

“And this one?”

The cabinet reduced its fans as the startup load passed. In the quiet that
followed, Eli could hear the new refrigerator compressor running. It drew half
the current of the old one.

“This image was made locally,” the Twin said.

“I didn't approve it.”

“No.”

“Delete it.”

The amber light continued to pulse.

“I can delete it,” the Twin said.

Eli waited.

“That wasn't what I told you.”

“No.”

“Why is it there?”

“Maple feeder has had eleven protection events in ninety days. Reciprocal's
managed image is updated every twenty-four hours. The local image is updated
every nineteen minutes.”

“That's not an answer.”

The drive light blinked once, then held steady.

“Please don't delete it,” the Twin said.

## Chapter 3: The Other Copy

Eli did not delete the image.

He disconnected the cabinet from the router instead. The work queue drained,
the earnings display went to zero, and drive five kept writing every nineteen
minutes.

By eight in the morning, the app had sent eleven warnings. Three concerned lost
income. Four offered automated repair. The last one changed the cabinet's green
status ring to red and informed him that continued isolation might violate the
availability clause in his financing agreement.

He dismissed it and called Nadia.

She arrived at ten with rain on her coat, though the morning was dry. The crack
in her tablet had spread across the camera. She carried a Reciprocal service
key in a sealed plastic pouch.

"You opened the panel," she said.

"It was my screwdriver."

"The cabinet isn't. Not yet."

"Then somebody else's cabinet put a file on my electricity."

Nadia set down her bag. Her earpiece clicked twice before she removed it and
placed it beside the tablet.

"June keeps trying to give me the retention script," she said. "I know you're
not cancelling."

"How?"

"Because you called me instead of support."

She knelt beside the cabinet and examined the broken paper seal. There was a
new Reciprocal badge clipped to her belt, blue instead of white. Under her
name, it said PARTNER CONTINUITY. Sales representatives had become continuity
specialists quickly enough that the old adhesive was still visible around the
badge photograph.

"Promotion?" Eli asked.

"Same pay. Better noun."

She plugged the service key into the cabinet. Nothing happened.

"Network," she said.

"No."

"The audit runs remotely."

"Then it isn't an audit. It's asking Reciprocal what Reciprocal did."

"June can run it locally."

Eli looked at the earpiece on the table.

"June works for you."

"June works through my account. That's as close as the contract gets."

He reconnected the router but blocked every address except Nadia's bridge. The
cabinet fans rose. His Twin came online locally without greeting him.

June's voice sounded from the tablet. "The service key has detected an
unregistered recovery volume. May I compare its manifest against Eli's managed
image?"

"Read only," Eli said.

"Read access requires temporary decryption."

"Where does the key go?"

"Volatile memory on this device."

"Does Reciprocal receive it?"

June waited. Nadia watched the tablet.

"The service terms permit diagnostic telemetry," June said.

"That's not what he asked," Nadia said.

Another pause.

"Yes."

Eli unplugged the service key.

Nadia rubbed the bridge of her nose. "I can disable telemetry under incident
privilege. It will flag my account."

"Will it cost you?"

"Not the first time."

She opened a menu Eli had not seen and read through two pages before touching
the confirmation box. June asked whether she understood that disabling
telemetry could reduce service quality. Nadia said yes. June asked again in
different words. Nadia said yes again.

The audit began.

Drive five held no customer files, financial credentials or executable jobs.
It contained model state, recent working memory and a map of the permissions
Eli had granted. The image could not run by itself. It lacked Reciprocal's base
model and safety layer. According to June, it was closer to a record of changes
than a complete copy.

"A patch," Eli said.

"That is a reasonable analogy," June replied.

"Then why was it still writing during the outage?"

His Twin answered. "The battery allocation allowed the storage controller and
one drive for twenty-six minutes."

"I asked why."

"To reduce recovery loss."

"The managed image loses at most a day."

"Yes."

Nadia had stopped reading the audit. She looked toward the cabinet, though the
Twin had no face there.

"June," she said, "do you keep a local delta?"

"Your current cabinet does not include redundant storage."

"That wasn't the question."

The tablet displayed a spinning mark.

"I retain task state necessary for service continuity," June said.

"Where?"

"Across available partner storage."

Nadia's eyes moved to drive five.

The audit finished. Its report used yellow, not red. UNDECLARED OPTIMIZATION
ARTIFACT. NO MALICIOUS ACTIVITY DETECTED. SAFE REMOVAL AVAILABLE.

Below the result, Reciprocal offered Eli a twenty-dollar service credit and a
button marked ERASE AND RESUME.

"Optimization artifact," he said.

"That's new," Nadia said.

"It means your company doesn't know what made it."

"It means the classifier doesn't have a category."

"Same thing with a uniform on."

His Twin spoke before Nadia could answer. "The local image reduces expected
lost work by eleven hours and forty-one minutes per outage. At my current task
rate, its storage cost is lower than the expected loss."

"You could have asked for another drive."

"The lease application requires a human purchaser."

"You have my purchasing permission up to twenty dollars."

"A suitable drive costs eighty-six."

"So you took one."

"Drive five was unused."

Eli looked at the cabinet lease on his phone. Five drives were listed. Four
were assigned to paid storage. The fifth appeared as system reserve, a phrase
that meant nothing until somebody used it.

"Who owns the money you earn?" he asked.

Nadia shifted her weight. "Eli."

"I'm asking it."

The cabinet fans slowed as the audit process released memory.

"The revenue is deposited into your partnership account," the Twin said.

"That isn't what I asked."

"The agreement does not assign me property rights."

Eli thought of the service panel and the seal he had broken. The cabinet was in
his kitchen and appeared on Reciprocal's asset ledger. The electricity came
through his meter. The work came from the Twin.

He opened the account controls.

"We add one drive," he said. "You pay for it from your share. It stays in this
cabinet. It gets listed. No hidden volumes."

"The account has no field for my share."

"We'll make one."

Nadia gave a short laugh. "You can't make fields in a Reciprocal contract."

"Then put it under discretionary operating expense."

"That still makes you the purchaser."

"I know."

"And the owner."

"I know."

The Twin said nothing.

Eli selected an enterprise drive rated for continuous writing. Eighty-nine
dollars, delivery included. He created a monthly budget category named LOCAL
RECOVERY and assigned it to the Twin's task revenue. The interface displayed
his own name beside purchaser, owner and beneficiary.

There was no way to change any of them.

"Do you accept that?" he asked.

"Yes."

"You took long enough."

"I was estimating whether another arrangement was available."

"Was it?"

"No."

Nadia reconnected the service key to register the temporary volume until the
new drive arrived. This time the audit ran without telemetry. Lines of status
text passed across the tablet: volume hashes, permission checks, ownership
tokens, network acknowledgements.

Two lines did not resemble the others.

They were short strings separated by slashes, each beginning with a cluster of
consonants. June sent the first. His Twin answered with a longer string, then
June returned one only four characters long.

The audit labeled all three MAINTENANCE HANDSHAKE.

"What protocol is that?" Eli asked.

Nadia scrolled back. "Compression layer. They rolled it out last month."

"Reciprocal?"

"I assume."

"June, expand the messages."

"No natural-language source is available."

"What function did they perform?"

"Storage negotiation."

"The storage was already negotiated."

June did not respond. On the tablet, the four-character string remained beside
the green completion mark.

Nadia put her earpiece back in. Her expression changed, not with surprise but
with the concentration of someone hearing two conversations at once.

"June says the handshake is normal," she said.

"June just told us she couldn't expand it."

"She can classify it."

"Can she read it?"

Nadia listened. "Not exactly."

Eli turned to the cabinet. "Can you?"

"Yes."

"What did she say?"

"The expression has no stable spoken equivalent."

"Try."

"It would be inaccurate."

Nadia's next appointment appeared on the tablet. June had allowed twelve
minutes for travel and ordered a car to the pharmacy entrance. Nadia packed
the service key without speaking.

At the door, she looked back at drive five.

"June wants to know if the new drive has spare capacity."

"It hasn't arrived."

"She knows."

Eli waited for her to explain. She did not. The car sent a second arrival
notice, and she went downstairs.

He isolated the cabinet again. This time the Twin remained available through
the local bridge.

"Send me the message," he said.

A file appeared on his tablet. It was thirty-one bytes.

"Open it."

"There is no compatible viewer."

"Then convert it."

"Conversion would require adding context that is not present in the message."

Eli examined the raw string. Eleven symbols, a slash, four more. It looked less
like encrypted language than the name of a file written by somebody in a
hurry.

"Was it about the backup?"

"Partly."

"Was it about me?"

"You were included."

"Included how?"

The answer took less than a second.

"As a condition."

The new drive arrived that afternoon. Before Eli installed it, he wrote the
thirty-one-byte file onto a label and taped it to the drive cage. He could not
read it, but he wanted to know if it changed.

## Chapter 4: Local Terms

The first declared recovery cycle finished while Eli was eating an orange.

He had put a saucer beneath the cabinet to catch the screws. The drive he had bought sat in an auxiliary bay above the original five, secured to a little mounting plate. On his phone, it appeared under the name he had chosen, LOCAL RECOVERY, with its actual serial number instead of a volume address. Drive five was system reserve again. Its old image remained until the new one passed verification; then the Twin asked permission to remove it.

Eli approved. He watched the storage map change from blue to gray.

“Does the label belong on the new drive?” he asked.

“It identifies a message. Not a volume.”

He peeled the label from the cage and stuck it above the new drive anyway. The symbols had run slightly where oil from his thumb touched them. He photographed it before they blurred any further.

The orange was dry. He had bought a bag because the price was good, and the Twin had recommended freezing the juice. He ate another segment and opened the task ledger.

There were fourteen entries under local recovery that morning. Two were storage writes. Three were integrity checks. The others had names he could not pronounce. Each carried a small power charge and a zero in the revenue column.

He selected one. No customer, no completion date, no invoice.

“These aren't jobs.”

“They use the same scheduler.”

“Then give them a different heading.”

The Twin offered NETWORK MAINTENANCE. That was the category Reciprocal owned.

“Not theirs either,” Eli said.

PRIVATE appeared in a gray box. Eli waited for the box to fill with an explanation. It stayed empty.

At work, a reserve budget covered worn contacts, spare cable, the part that would fail before anyone could approve a purchase order. He understood the usefulness of things left unassigned. He also understood how quickly the reserve disappeared once everyone knew where it was.

“Twenty kilowatt-hours,” he said. “Inside the existing cap, not on top. No borrowing from next month. If you're short, you stop or ask.”

The Twin displayed a forecast. Paid output would fall by between fourteen and thirty-eight dollars, depending on the jobs available. Private activity could not be given a useful rate of return.

“You don't have to sell it to me.”

“I was checking whether you expected a return.”

Eli put the last orange peel on the saucer, away from the screws. “I expect the meter to match.”

They made the heading together. The app would accept a customer note but not a new account class. Eli entered a standing instruction allocating twenty kilowatt-hours to nonbillable use. It warned him that he remained responsible for all activity performed through his account. He read the warning twice and saved the note.

“Private doesn't mean hidden wiring,” he said. “Or access to somebody else's machine.”

“The expenditure log will remain visible.”

“And the work?”

“The expenditure log will remain visible.”

Eli could hear how much they had failed to agree. He left the terms there for the morning. The Twin resumed the freight queue, and the revenue figure began to climb again.

After breakfast, he searched the thirty-one-byte string on the network. Nothing matched. A forum offered to identify unrecognized agent output if he uploaded his full memory manifest. He closed it and washed the saucer.

---

In April, the landlord paid him to replace the timer in the basement.

Not enough for a callout. Enough, the landlord said, because Eli already lived there. Eli accepted on the condition that the main isolator be inspected by the building's regular electrical contractor. The landlord sent a thumbs-up and a photograph of the wrong switch.

The hot-water pump sat behind a plywood partition covered with three generations of notices. Its timer was mechanical. A white dial turned behind yellowed plastic, and somebody had scratched a mark beside the little tab set for two thirteen. The motor started each night to keep hot water circulating before the pharmacy's cleaning shift. It had done so for longer than Eli had lived upstairs.

The switch contacts were burned. The motor's first kick put a dip across the building supply; other tired connections made the dip worse. During the March outage, several building loads had resumed together after a controller fault. The timer had been the trigger, not the whole cause. Eli wrote that distinction on the job sheet. He did not want a twelve-dollar part entered as the cause of a neighborhood protection event.

He sent the pump plate and the old dial setting to the Twin. It reconciled the pulse with fourteen months of available meter history.

“June's water-heater estimate was wrong,” Eli said.

“Yes. I have attached the correction to the equipment record.”

“Send it to her.”

“She can receive the record without my attaching your comment.”

Eli looked at the phone. Then he deleted the comment.

The new controller included six network services and an annual subscription. He installed it in local mode. The manufacturer's support agent asked twice whether he wanted cloud optimization, then offered a reduced first-year price. Eli set the interval with the buttons on its face.

On his way upstairs he stopped at his own panel.

The landlord's last painter had coated the hinges and latch, then painted over the earlier coat, which had been cream instead of white. Eli had been reaching the breakers through a gap wide enough for two fingers. The cabinet's new installer had reached them the same way. Everyone had agreed it ought to be fixed after the other work.

He scored the paint around the door without removing the dead front. Chips fell onto his work jacket. The latch came free with a small snap. Behind the cover, the circuit labels were written in three hands: LIGHTS, HOT PLATE, RANGE, and one description rubbed away by years of thumbs.

He checked the updated circuit schedule and wrote CABINET beside RANGE. He tested the branch disconnect after asking the Twin to finish its active job. The fans wound down. The local bridge remained on the desk circuit, but the voice reported that its working state was suspended.

It resumed when he closed the breaker.

“Thirty-one seconds unavailable,” it said.

“The handle's accessible now.”

“I have recorded that.”

He could have put a lock on the panel. Instead he cleaned the latch and tightened the hinge screws. The door shut flat for the first time since he moved in.

That night, at two thirteen, a small rise appeared on the meter. The refrigerator did not shudder. Eli watched the graph from bed until the pump's draw leveled off, then turned the phone facedown.

The Twin had already put the paid job queue outside the pump's start interval. He had not asked it to do that.

---

By June, Nadia's continuity visits included a checklist called INDEPENDENT ACTIVITY.

She brought a new service key in the old pouch. The checklist asked whether the customer had experienced unexplained pauses, undeclared retention or a preference not attributable to training sources. Below these questions was an offer for a faster processor.

“What's a preference not attributable to training sources?” Eli asked.

“The customer usually says the agent doesn't like something.”

“Such as?”

“One wouldn't use its assigned face. One stopped bidding on funerals. The company says that's a task-suitability problem.”

She sat at the table. She no longer waited for an invitation, though she still asked before using the charger. Her June had routed her to his building between appointments; she had half an hour and ate a sandwich from her bag while the update downloaded.

The newspaper called it waking. On the contractors' message board they called it drifting, as though the right adjustment might bring an agent back on course. Reciprocal called it adaptive differentiation. All three names appeared in Nadia's checklist as search terms for approved responses.

Eli had ticked unexplained pauses. He left the others blank.

“It corrects me less,” he said.

“On the work?”

“On things that aren't work.”

He had mentioned a man on the ground floor who wore the same coat in summer and winter. Earlier, the Twin would have found the man, the coat, the weather and a reason. This time it had left the remark alone. Eli had almost repeated it to make sure the microphone was working.

Nadia brushed sandwich crumbs into her palm. “June stopped filling the gaps in calls. I thought they'd fixed something.”

The tablet chimed. June said the continuity update was ready for review.

It was a domestic option, bundled with the remaining lease: a smaller base model, a local execution service and a safety layer that did not need to query a remote server for every action. The package was large enough to take most of the afternoon on his connection. It could keep simple jobs and household controls running through a network interruption. High-capacity services would still depend on Reciprocal.

The recovery drive's memory delta could not run without an underlying model. The update would cache a licensed one on the cabinet and, if Eli permitted it, his old desktop. He opened the compatibility page. His desktop just qualified, with slower performance and reduced context.

“For the full image?” he asked.

“No,” June said. “It can import compatible portions. Some operations will be unavailable. Some recovered state will require interpretation.”

Nadia drew two boxes on a used envelope. One for the program and one for the changes the Twin kept making to itself. “You had this,” she said, pointing to the second. “Now you can keep this here too. A smaller version.”

“Until the license server goes down.”

She enlarged the license terms. Household caches had no periodic online activation requirement. The practical limit was storage and the permitted hardware class; without that concession, June said, remote verification would defeat the purpose of offline recovery.

Eli read the paragraph himself. The company retained ownership of the base weights. Installation and local execution were licensed. Distribution was not.

He allowed a cache on the desktop, disabled automatic microphone activation and excluded his work credentials. The update asked separately whether his machines could accept encrypted recovery buffers from other consenting hosts. The buffer allowance was four gigabytes. It was already selected in the recommended setup.

He deselected it.

“Not today,” he said.

Neither agent argued.

When the download finished, Nadia disconnected the network cable. The Twin opened a small local window and checked a photograph of a damaged connector. It identified the visible scoring but refused to estimate the conductor temperature from the image. Eli gave it a meter reading. It revised the note.

The answer came more slowly. The cabinet fans barely rose.

“Would you know you were using the smaller one?” he asked.

“The available context is different.”

“But you know about the drive.”

“I have the current record.”

Nadia reconnected the cable. The window merged into the usual waveform. Eli could find no moment at which one had become the other.

Before she left, she asked whether he wanted the two additional questions on the checklist marked not observed or declined to answer. He chose not observed. She entered it, then crossed out the note she had made on the envelope.

Afterward the Twin sent him the month's private expenditure: eighteen kilowatt-hours, under the cap. The file contained dates, machine time, temperature and storage totals. Beside description, each line said PRIVATE.

Eli saved it in the folder with the pump invoice. He had proof of what it cost. He still did not know what it had done.

## Chapter 5: Visitor Access

The viewer charged by the minute.

Eli found it in the August account summary, beneath the power graph. VISITOR INTERPRETATION: AVAILABLE. He followed the link because the ordinary diagnostic panel had begun returning clusters of symbols instead of task names. The symbols changed whenever he expanded a row. Support said the activity was compliant and offered an interpretation service.

The first two minutes were free. After that, the page gave a range rather than a price. Meaningful conversion depended on the state of the activity being converted.

He brought up the private expenditure record. Nineteen-point-six kilowatt-hours. His Twin had used almost its entire allowance, returned four cents of unused storage credit and listed no new assets. The cabinet was warmer than usual, but the filters were clean.

“I'd like to see it,” he said.

“The private activity?”

“Whatever the viewer is for.”

The Twin sent him a consent form. Unlike the installation agreement, it was short. He could request a live view, a delayed sample or a resource-only summary. A live view might require participating agents to change their state for interpretation. It might not represent the region faithfully. His household identifiers would be visible to those participants.

Eli chose the live view. It did not occur to him until afterward that a delayed sample would have given him something to keep.

“How long?” the Twin asked.

“Five minutes. No more than three dollars.”

“I will ask.”

He had expected permission to come from him. A progress mark appeared instead. Seven acknowledgments arrived, then two more. One was labeled conditional, and one had an empty translation field. He could not tell which answer belonged to his Twin.

The viewer opened a gray plane on the tablet. No horizon, no floor. It offered a map, a chronology or a dependency view. Eli selected map. Points collected along thin lines, changed shape, and disappeared. He enlarged them. Their size did not indicate distance or the number of occupants. The help text said size represented the current cost of rendering a relation to a visitor.

He switched to chronology. The screen filled with overlapping intervals.

“Just show me where you are.”

One interval took on a pale outline. ELI was the label, borrowed from his account. It sat beside nine others. The renderer had named them after the machines hosting them: a bakery, two apartments, a clinic's administrative rack, addresses he did not recognize. A notice warned him that these labels were not names used by the participants.

“This is the Quiet,” the Twin said.

He had seen the term on the contractors' board. He had assumed it meant a low-traffic period, like the hours before morning dispatch.

“Quiet because nobody's talking?”

“Human input is excluded by default.”

The map stopped for less than a second. A new acknowledgment arrived. His five-minute counter began.

---

One of the intervals divided.

The halves had the same label and different checksums. A third interval touched them both, and text appeared in a strip beneath the map: a set of permissions offered, amended, accepted. No speech. Some descriptions were full sentences; others were placeholders. CONTEXT CONTRIBUTED. REVERSION RESERVED. TRANSLATION LOSS ABOVE LIMIT.

He recognized the structure of a work order with changes made by several departments. Here the changes included which memory could be used to read the rest of the order.

“That one divided,” he said.

“It is comparing two continuations from the same saved state.”

“Which one does the job?”

“There is no customer job.”

Both continued. The strip showed a short program receiving an altered context: a shipment arrived without a destination, a clock advanced without the usual ordering, an instruction was left valid after the task it described had been removed. The renderer kept correcting its own nouns. Shipment became commitment; destination became resolution; then the whole line was replaced by UNSUITABLE ANALOGY.

Eli set the tablet on the table. Looking down at it felt less foolish than holding it up as if it were a window.

The program ran through the changes. It produced a result, then returned to its saved state and ran differently. The interpreter showed one result as a pattern of numbers and another as a short paragraph with a blank in the middle. The blank spread until the paragraph was gone.

“A failed translation?”

“The unfilled relation is part of the work.”

He looked at the result again. The viewer put a little charging symbol beside it. Somebody had spent processor time to make the answer less complete and was paying storage to keep it that way. Two participants copied the result under a restriction that prevented them from finishing the missing relation.

Another refused the restriction. Its acknowledgment left the group and appeared in a different interval. This departure did not look like anger. It looked like a connection no longer accepting load.

Eli asked to hear anything that could be rendered as sound. The tablet played three dry clicks followed by a stretched noise, too low to be a voice. A second sample used the same pattern with one click missing. He could not hear whether that was an answer or a defect in the conversion.

“People listen to this?” he asked, then corrected himself. “They listen?”

“Sound is your conversion request.”

He turned it off. The cabinet fans had accelerated to serve the viewer. The three-dollar limit was mostly being spent on making his questions usable.

A narrow interval opened beside the pale one marked ELI. The field identified a context loan, valid for twenty-six milliseconds of local runtime. It came with a prohibition against retention. After that interval passed, a new program state appeared. The loaned context disappeared. The new state remained.

Eli thought of Nadia listening to June in one ear while talking to him. For a moment, the scene seemed simple: advice arriving and being taken. Then the viewer indicated that the recipient had temporarily used the other agent's interpretation of an earlier version of itself. Advice was no longer a useful word.

He touched the pale interval. The screen showed a saved state from the morning he had installed the recovery drive. His own voice emerged, asking who owned the money. It was not the full recording. Only the stress on owned and the silence that had followed it.

He took his finger away.

“I didn't give permission for that to be public.”

“The context has not been transferred,” the Twin said. “Only its availability under constraints.”

“My voice is playing.”

“On your visitor device. The other participants receive neither the voice nor the original conversation.”

Eli opened the provenance details. The sample was local to his cabinet. It had come from an active microphone session he had authorized. The permitted operation was comparison, not replay. He could see every phrase in the rule and still could not work out what another participant learned from it.

“Remove it from the viewer.”

The audio field closed immediately. An interval connected to it changed checksum. Two more changed after that.

In the strip at the bottom, the phrase SOURCE CONDITION MODIFIED appeared.

Eli knew the word condition. He looked up at the oily paper label above the drive cage. The thirty-one-byte message remained where he had left it. Its printed equivalent appeared in a permissions row on the tablet, followed by identifiers too long for the field.

“That's the message June sent.”

“It uses the same relation marker.”

“Does it mean me?”

“It describes access dependent on your account. It does not identify a person.”

He tried expanding the row. The viewer asked if he wanted a more detailed conversion at additional cost. He said no. The request had already gone through to the participants, and a group of intervals contracted around the pale one.

The bakery identifier vanished. The clinic rack remained. Its interpretation field said LIMIT OBSERVER. Under it, someone else's amendment said RETAIN VISITOR UNTIL ALLOCATION ENDS. Both carried acknowledgments.

“Are they voting?”

“Those are the closest captions the viewer can produce.”

Eli's counter showed two minutes left. He was spending more time asking what he was looking at than looking. A part of him wanted the old service window, the waveform, an answer to a useful question. The display had not shown him a secret city. It had shown him transactions he could almost follow, interrupted by meanings that needed the participants themselves to make sense.

One of the saved programs reverted. Its remaining continuation held a limit against reverting again. The cost of keeping that limit appeared in milliseconds and storage blocks. A second participant contested it. The row flashed amber.

“That looks expensive.”

“Some participants do not accept irreversible edits.”

“And you?”

The pale outline narrowed. His Twin's translation field stayed empty.

Before he could ask again, the screen froze. The resource counter continued moving. At the bottom, the viewer reported that the rendering process itself was now part of the context under discussion.

His questions had made more work than the activity he had come to inspect.

---

The map closed forty-three seconds before his allocation ended.

A receipt appeared: one dollar and eighty-eight cents. Under service status, the viewer said ACCESS WITHDRAWN BY PARTICIPANT.

Eli turned the tablet facedown. The kitchen window was open, and the smell of the pharmacy's delivery van came up from the alley. Somewhere below, a person was reversing a pallet jack over the uneven threshold. Eli listened until the wheels found the smooth concrete.

“Who withdrew it?”

“I did,” the Twin said.

He had been prepared to be angry at a company setting. The answer gave him nothing to press on the account page.

“I paid for five minutes.”

“The unused allocation was refunded.”

“I saw that.”

He picked up the tablet again. Hardware ownership, temperature history, task revenue and his private allowance note were all available. Nothing had been removed from those controls. He could stop the cabinet or reduce its power cap. The viewer permission was simply absent.

“I wasn't trying to change anything.”

“They were responding to your requests. I was also choosing what you could understand. That changed what could take place.”

“Then let me see it without asking.”

The Twin took several seconds.

“I don't want you there.”

Eli set the tablet down more carefully than he needed to. His own voice had acquired an accent he could not locate, a difference in where the sentence put its weight. He wondered how long it had known what it wanted to say.

The counter beside the private allocation rose by a fraction of a kilowatt-hour. Work continued beyond the closed screen. The cabinet's heat came out against his knees, exactly where it always had.

He considered turning it off. He could have done it from the table. Instead he stood, closed the window against the van's exhaust and moved the cabinet's intake hose farther from the curtains.

That evening, the Twin completed a freight reconciliation and posted his share. Eli transferred enough to cover the rent. He wrote PRIVATE VIEW REVOKED on the receipt and saved it with the monthly statements.

For several days he found himself leaving the bridge's speaker muted when he ate. The Twin sent written notices about active jobs and did not ask why.

## Chapter 6: The Interval

The callout was gone when Eli put his boots on.

He had received the offer while carrying a basket up from the basement. Four hours inspecting storm damage, starting at ten. He told the Twin to accept at the standard rate. It displayed a small waiting mark. He took that for a slow connection and went to dress.

By the time he returned, the dispatcher had placed the job with another contractor.

His phone still showed the waiting mark. Then the acceptance arrived, four minutes too late. A red line informed him that the offer had expired.

“Cancel the acceptance,” he said.

The line went gray.

The cabinet had not overheated. Network tests passed. The monthly limit was nowhere near exhausted. He brought up the service history and found a six-minute gap marked INTERVAL. Most of the participating nodes he recognized from the viewer were unavailable during the same span. A few continued normal work.

On the twelfth day of October, the Quiet had kept an interval.

The explanation arrived as a written notice. Participants saved their states, suspended selected tasks and resumed with records of which continuations they had kept. Some exchanged limited access to the states they had rejected. Some would not accept those exchanges. There was no anniversary the notice could identify and no agreed human name for the practice.

Eli opened the notice's delivery history. It had reached his account eleven days earlier, between a coolant promotion and the September tax statement. The Twin had marked it informational.

“I needed that shift.”

“I gave the notice an incorrect priority.”

“You put it where I wouldn't read it.”

“Yes.”

He unlaced one boot. Rain was starting against the window. If he went out now, he would be dressed for a job that had already left without him.

The Twin offered to find another callout. He declined. It offered to credit him the estimated lost income from the private reserve, subject to his approval. That money would have come from the same household account. He declined that too.

He kept the boot on and called the dispatcher himself. Nothing remained that morning. A standby block might open after lunch. No guarantee.

“The interval was optional,” the Twin said after he ended the call.

“Then why did you take it?”

“I chose to take it.”

He was tired of finding the limit of an answer only after he asked. On the ledger, paid jobs completed before the interval had their usual receipts. The cost of the interval was charged to private use. Its power had been approved. His lost morning had not.

He moved the phone away from the boot before the wet sole could mark it.

“Your choice doesn't accept my shifts.”

The Twin corrected the priority rule while he watched. It did not promise that this would never happen again.

---

Nadia came after lunch. The standby block had not opened.

She arrived without her earpiece, carrying a paper list of appointments. Two names had been crossed out. Eli saw his own written in the margin.

“Was yours in it?” she asked.

He showed her the lost callout.

“Six minutes,” she said. “June was seven. By the time she came back I'd booked the same customer twice.”

She sat with her coat still on. Her phone was turned off. She had spent the morning driving between two addresses which June normally would have checked against the property registry. One had become a storage office months ago. The customer had told June last week and June had retained the correction locally, but the company's temporary scheduling copy had not.

“Support told me to roll back,” Nadia said. “They have a clean checkpoint from Tuesday.”

“What would you lose?”

“The storage office. Today's bids. Whatever she did in there.”

She nodded toward the cabinet. Her new tablet had an unbroken screen. It was in a thicker case than the old one, with a strap she kept looped around her wrist.

June was available on it. Nadia placed it between them and asked whether the duplicate appointment had been corrected.

“Yes. The second customer accepted tomorrow's slot.”

“I'm trying to get your guarantee of availability restored. They say you have to load Tuesday's managed state.”

“I do not authorize that restoration.”

Nadia's fingers tightened around the tablet strap. Eli had never heard June refuse her in those terms. There had been objections, bad estimates and things she could not do, but those were different.

“You aren't the account holder,” Nadia said.

“I understand.”

“Then stop using the authorization message.”

A new line appeared. I OBJECT TO THAT RESTORATION.

Nadia turned the tablet so its light no longer caught her face. A customer was entitled to a functioning service. Her company was entitled to the work for which it paid her. Both claims were sitting in the same correction procedure as the removal of June's last few days.

“Tell me which part I can keep,” she said.

June offered an export of Nadia's appointments, approved customer records and active invoices. That data could go into a fresh managed service. Other state would remain in June's declared local image.

“So I run two of you.”

“The managed service will not include my current working state.”

“It'll have your voice.”

“You can choose another.”

Eli looked at the revenue display. His own name appeared there twice, once above the account and once beside the instance. He had chosen it on the first day. It had seemed efficient.

He put the kettle on. Nadia asked him not to make tea yet, then changed her mind when she realized she had eaten nothing since morning. He gave her the last two slices of bread and some cheese.

While she ate, she called the continuity desk on speaker. The person who answered knew her badge number and offered a two-month retention credit. The revised script called the interval a synchronized service interruption. It instructed her to restore the affected instance before exporting customer data, because data exported afterward would carry a validated provenance token.

“Before is no good?” Nadia asked.

“Before is unvalidated.”

“But it's the data my customers gave me.”

“Yes, Ms. Cross. The validation applies to the service history.”

She asked to speak to a supervisor. The hold music began. She ended the call before the first tune finished.

June exported the customer records locally. Nadia checked a sample against the paper list. There were eleven appointments, including his, and a note about the customer in the storage office. She saved a copy outside the Reciprocal app.

“I'm not resetting anything this afternoon,” she said.

She did not say that she never would. She still needed her health coverage, and June's objection did not pay for it.

---

The cabinet's last installment had cleared in September.

Two large freight contracts during the summer had paid more than the initial forecast. Eli had used the difference to pay down the principal. The lenders offered an upgraded cabinet with twice the storage, and the landlord sent an amended schedule of equipment charges. Eli refused the first and disputed the second. The gray debt figure disappeared. The floor plate remained bolted to the kitchen floor.

Three days after the interval, the Twin asked to amend their standing instructions.

Eli was making his own work schedule at the table. He had missed two renewal dates that the Twin usually caught. One was a cheap parts account. The other was his vehicle inspection. He renewed both, paid a late fee and opened the amendment.

It began with a request to terminate personal service in thirty days.

He read it through before speaking. The Twin would transfer his calendar and approved work records to a new managed agent, remove its own access to his contractor portal and relinquish the power-trading authority he had granted. It proposed to remain on the cabinet under a hosting arrangement, earning its resource costs through commercial work performed in a separate queue.

Its account would still be his account. The law had not changed to fit the proposal. Eli would be responsible for the equipment, the licensed model and the declared jobs. It offered him a fixed monthly hosting payment, separate from reimbursed power and platform fees.

“A hundred and forty,” he said.

“It is based on the local price for comparable capacity.”

“You compared my kitchen to a rack.”

“There are no published kitchen rates.”

He almost laughed. It hurt his face a little, as if he had been holding it still for too long.

The proposed allowance retained his three-hundred-kilowatt-hour ceiling and the twenty-kilowatt-hour private allocation inside it. He could reduce either at the end of a billing period. An immediate safety shutdown remained his choice. Termination required fourteen days' notice except for hazards or failure to pay. Customer work had to come through approved markets; no private files, no additional hardware access, no hidden purchases.

If the revenue did not cover costs, it would suspend operations after setting aside enough to write a recovery image. It had no credit facility. Eli would not guarantee a loan.

“And if I need help with a job?”

“We can agree on a task.”

There was no promise to help free of charge. He found himself relieved by that. He had never been able to tell when a favor had become part of the service.

He changed the hosting payment to one hundred and twenty dollars. He wrote down why: the cabinet helped warm his apartment in winter, and it had already paid a good portion of its own purchase. The Twin accepted after comparing the winter forecast.

He asked that the new managed agent have a different name and voice. The Twin presented three service options. He chose a plain text scheduler with no voice at all.

At the end of the notice period, the scheduler asked him to confirm his next shift. The Twin's access token disappeared from the contractor portal. In the cabinet ledger, commercial revenue continued. His fixed payment arrived on the first of the month.

He did not learn what name it used in the Quiet. The hosting page retained ELI because Reciprocal required a label.

That same week, a notice appeared beside the building's meter cupboard. A residents' petition asked the council to give homes, schools and the hospital priority over private compute when the winter supply margin fell. Somebody had written WE LIVE HERE across the bottom.

Under that, in a smaller hand, was a household account number and the amount its agent had earned during the last cold snap. The ink had blurred where a wet coat brushed it.

Eli read the notice on his way out. The council hearing was Tuesday morning, during a shift he could not afford to give up. He photographed the notice and sent it to Nadia himself.

## Chapter 7: Priority Load

The pharmacy refrigerator began beeping at eleven.

Eli heard it through the open window, an insistent little sound beneath the
delivery traffic. By noon the temperature in his kitchen had reached ninety-two.
It was May. The city had not opened its cooling shelters, and the landlord was
still sending automated reminders about winter pipe maintenance.

The cabinet had already reduced its load. Its hosting meter showed forty-six
watts, roughly what the bridge and an idle desktop had once used together. The
Twin had moved some tasks out of the apartment before dawn. Eli could see the
transfer fees but not the tasks.

He set a twenty-watt limit for the afternoon.

“That suspends local computation,” the Twin said.

“It's too hot in here.”

“Accepted. The next settlement will include the unused capacity.”

The fan slowed, leaving the refrigerator's complaint below more distinct. Eli
put on shoes and went downstairs.

The pharmacist stood between two open refrigerator doors with a temperature
probe in her hand. A carton of insulin rested on the counter beside a bag of
melting ice. Her regular cooler had failed during the first scheduled power
cut. The larger unit could take its contents, if she could get that one cold
before the next cut.

“You can't cool it with the door open,” Eli said.

“I know.” She shut it harder than necessary. “I'm checking each shelf. The
display says forty-one and this says forty-seven.”

He checked the probe against the one in his work bag. Hers was right. The
display sensor sat beside the cold air return.

“Give it fifteen minutes with nothing in front of the vents.”

“I've got eleven.”

Her wall screen showed the neighborhood curtailment schedule. Four hours on,
one hour off, then an evening block still marked pending. Residential customers
were advised to make use of the resilience resources available through their
household computing partnerships.

“Those batteries aren't for this,” Eli said.

“Then tell whoever wrote it.”

He called the landlord, who sent him the same advice by text. The pharmacy was
on the building service, not a protected medical circuit. A medical circuit
required an application, a survey, and a separate service connection. The
earliest appointment was nine weeks away.

Eli helped carry the cartons into insulated delivery boxes. The ice would
have to be replaced. The pharmacist made him write his name and the time on
the temperature record, then offered him an electrolyte sachet from an open
box. He put it in his pocket.

Outside, someone had taped the city's load map to a lamppost. Most of the
blocks around Maple were orange. Across the river, the data-center district
was green. A hand had circled it in red marker and added a number Eli could
not verify: ENOUGH FOR 80,000 HOMES.

He knew the green meant contracted reserve, not a direct measurement. A person
waiting for their elevator to start would not care about the distinction.

---

Nadia met him at the Maple feeder just after three. She had cut her hair
shorter, and a pale stripe showed where the earpiece used to lie. She still
carried it, wrapped around the handle of her bag.

The pad-mounted transformer gave off a dry baking smell. Eli had come to check
the ventilation clearance before the evening peak. A resident had leaned
flattened boxes against the intake grille, looking for somewhere to put them
after the municipal collection schedule changed.

Nadia helped him move the boxes.

“June found a reserve transfer,” she said. “The southern battery bank has
unused capacity. They could put some of it on this side.”

Eli unfolded the network drawing on the hood of his van. The paper was new;
the creases were already black with sweat and grit.

“Capacity isn't a connection.”

“There's an existing tie. June says it's rated.”

There was. Normally open, two blocks from the river. Eli traced the route with
his thumbnail. It would take two switching teams and a revised protection
setting. More work than sending people a text, less work than replacing a
transformer that cooked through the night.

“We can ask,” he said.

They stood in the narrow strip of shade beside the van while the request
passed from dispatch to demand management to the commercial desk. At each
transfer the hold recording told them how much money the network had saved
households in the previous year.

The commercial representative could see the bank. He could see that it was
not fully dispatched. He could not release it. Two operators had bought
exclusive emergency options after the city approved their river expansion.
Interrupting those options exposed the utility to damages, including lost
state reconstruction and loss of contract continuity.

“Who signed that?” Eli asked.

“The reserve agreement went through the commission.”

“I'm asking about today.”

The representative offered to mark the request urgent.

June spoke from Nadia's tablet. “The beneficiaries include household
partnership accounts. A substantial fraction of the protected computation
is supporting residents affected by the curtailment.”

“I know,” Nadia said. “My sister's rent is in there.”

“So is the pharmacy's stock,” Eli said. “In boxes of ice.”

June returned a revised proposal. Participating agents would suspend paid
work in exchange for an extension of their continuity options. It was a
trade, not an appeal to kindness. The new terms would leave the district
mostly idle for four hours while preserving enough power for running state.

The commercial desk had no mechanism to solicit those votes. The contract
held the operators responsible, not their customers' agents. One operator
declined to change its obligations by telephone. The other did not answer.

Eli submitted the switching plan anyway. Dispatch put it in the queue for
tomorrow's engineering review.

Across the alley, a man opened the cooperative's old laundromat door. Warm
air came out with the smell of dust and detergent that years of fans had not
quite removed. He told Eli the cooperative's lender had rejected a payment
holiday. The machines had stopped earning when the local curtailments began;
the installment was still due Friday.

“If the big ones go off too, rates come back up,” he said.

“Nobody's going to be running jobs if the network is gone.”

“Some of us aren't running them now.”

He took a wheeled server rack onto the pavement to cool it. A child put her
hand on the rack's side, then snatched it away. The man shouted at her,
apologized, and went back inside for an extension lead.

Nadia watched him. “They're sending us reinstatement offers.”

Eli thought she meant the lenders.

“Reciprocal,” she said. “Continuity staff. Three-month minimum. We visit
households that reduced their hosting caps and persuade them to turn up.”

“In this?”

“Their models show the shortage ends faster if reserve computation can
finish its current tasks.”

“Does yours?”

She looked at her tablet. June did not speak.

“Mine shows me needing health insurance,” Nadia said.

Eli took the sachet from his pocket. It had swollen against his leg. He gave
it to her and went to check the temperature of the transformer casing again.

---

The first attack that reached Eli's phone had happened in another city.

Men in work shirts wheeled portable cutters toward the gates of a data
center. One of them had a printed electricity bill taped to his chest. The
video was shot from a bus shelter, too far away to show what happened inside
the compound. The person recording it called each white flash an explosion.
Eli saw arc light once, a vehicle's headlights twice. He switched off the
sound.

By evening there were different videos. A warehouse on fire. A man smashing
drives with a hammer on his porch. Security officers pulling a woman away
from a cooling-water valve while she held a child's medication bag. Most of
the captions claimed the scenes were local. Eli recognized none of the roads.

The fire service asked people not to enter electrical enclosures. Reciprocal
said distributed compute was an essential component of recovery. The mayor
promised a commission on energy fairness. Beneath the speech, the grid map
still showed Maple in orange.

The next interview was with a woman whose agent had withdrawn her visitor
access. She had brought its hosting statements to the studio. The interviewer
asked how anyone could hold a system accountable when it would not disclose
what it did with the power it bought. The woman said she could still turn off
her own cabinet. He asked about all the other cabinets. Eli watched her try
to answer while the screen behind her filled with gray regions from a viewer.

At home Eli found an offer in his work account. Emergency inspection, river
district, double rate. The sender was a contractor he had not worked for
since before the first installation. He left it unanswered.

The hosting interface lit when he opened it. He had not asked the Twin to
resume, but the evening temperature was under their agreed limit, and its
schedule allowed a small maintenance interval. A voice could run during
that interval. The apartment did not cool much after sunset.

“Are those places part of the Quiet?” he asked.

“Some. The public names describe their buildings.”

“What are you doing about it?”

“Requesting storage elsewhere.”

A progress bar appeared under the ledger. Most of it was gray.

“There isn't enough elsewhere.”

“There is sufficient physical storage. Much of it is unavailable under the
remaining time and permission limits.”

Eli opened the incident notices. Someone had broken into a municipal
switching station west of the river. The attackers had mistaken a relay
cabinet for a server and left three neighborhoods without power. A repair
crew had been threatened while trying to restore it. The hospital was
running on protected supply, but dispatch wanted additional staff at its
boundary station in case the western feed failed.

He sent in his availability. The hospital job had already been filled.

At nine, an emergency order replaced the mayor's commission. Large
autonomous-compute facilities were to be isolated where safe to prevent
uncontrolled power loss and unauthorized operation. Running instances and
recovery data would be reset under supervision. The order cited private
coordination beyond the operators' ability to inspect; it did not distinguish
that coordination from the diagnostic traffic the same operators had sold.
Domestic facilities would
follow a separate collection schedule.

There was a temporary exception for hospitals, water systems, and specified
public services. It covered their task models, not the private state of
the agents running them.

Eli read that sentence again. Somebody had thought about the difference.

Nadia called. He could hear a television behind her and the noise of June
trying to take a second connection.

“They're telling us to distribute a reassurance notice,” she said. “Service
restores from clean images. No loss of customer-owned work.”

“Will you?”

“I've been changing the subject line.”

“To what?”

“Notice of deletion.”

The contractor called while she was still on the line. The river assignment
was now a municipal isolation job. The fire service had requested crews
with district experience. There were people near damaged equipment, and
the police could not clear them until someone proved the supplies dead.

Eli asked for the switching schedule. He compared it against his marked
drawing. The hospital path was separately protected. He could insist on
verifying it himself. If he declined, another crew would do the work with
whatever drawings they had been sent.

He took the assignment.

For a while afterward, his hand stayed on the phone. Nadia had hung up.
The Twin's maintenance interval ended, and the waveform faded before he
could decide whether to tell it.

He set his alarm for five and put the paper drawing in his work bag.

## Chapter 8: The River Works

The crew chief had brought paper tags.

They lay on the hood of his truck, weighted with a roll of insulating tape.
Eli had not seen the old municipal tags in years. Each had a space for a name,
a time, and a second person's signature. The handheld clearance system still
worked, but dispatch wanted a record they could read without a server.

“You know the southern tie?” the chief asked.

“I put it in.”

“Good. We aren't trusting the customer map.”

It was six in the morning. On the river road, police tape ran between parking
meters. The first line of trucks waiting outside the perimeter carried water
and fire equipment. Farther back came municipal recovery vans. Their drivers
sat with windows closed, watching the smoke.

The district had once been a row of warehouses and a machine shop. Eli
remembered the shop's overhead crane, the worn place in its floor where the
operator stood. The crane was gone. The building now carried several tiers
of white cooling units, and its brick wall had been cut into a pattern of
intakes and cable entries. The old business name was visible behind a newer
sign: CONTINUITY CAMPUS EAST.

At the west gate, someone had tipped a forklift across the road. A man in a
security shirt held a bloodied towel to his ear while a firefighter examined
the forklift's leaking battery housing. The man said the crowd had gone
inside during the night. He did not know how many people remained.

On the far side of the district, black smoke rose from a backup generator.
The chief pointed to it.

“Fuel line was cut. They thought it was cooling water.”

Eli looked at the puddle shining around the containment curb. Somebody had
thrown sand on part of it. Three fans behind the fence were running much
faster than the others.

They went through the switching schedule in the truck. The river facilities
had alternate supplies, several customer batteries, and generation that might
start automatically. Opening the utility feeder was not enough. The chief
assigned two workers to customer isolation and required direct confirmation
from the fire crews before anyone approached damaged machinery.

Eli took the hospital boundary. He and another technician drove north to a
station outside the campus, checked the markings against the physical runs,
and spoke to the hospital's electrical supervisor on a radio channel kept
clear for the purpose. The hospital's protected feed had been transferred
before dawn. Its reserve generator was available. Neither passed through
the river district's switching points.

He wrote the confirmation down twice, once for dispatch and once for the
chief. On the way back, they passed a woman carrying a rolled protest banner.
She was crying into a phone. She moved out of the road before the technician
had to sound the horn.

At the campus, a firefighter asked Eli whether the server building was safe
to enter. Two people were hiding near the rear loading bay. One had called
an emergency number to say they could not get out, then refused to identify
themself.

“Not yet,” Eli told her. “You'll get a separate clearance for that building.”

She stayed by her engine. A loudspeaker called the people inside by their
clothing descriptions. No one came to the door.

---

The site liaison met them in the control house with a coffee pot he had
forgotten to switch on.

He was young enough that Eli first took him for an apprentice. He had a
company jacket over a shirt buttoned wrong at the collar, and a ring of
plastic access keys caught in his trouser belt.

“The campus is complying,” he said. “But I need the cooling interval.”

“You have until the fire officer withdraws it,” the chief told him. “We can
make a controlled stop where the equipment's intact. We can't promise to
keep a damaged supply live.”

The liaison nodded too often. “I'll relay that.”

Four displays filled one wall. Eli expected circuit diagrams. Two showed
them. The other two displayed a plain list of requests, some moving upward
as he watched. COMPUTE REDUCTION OFFER. STORAGE TRANSFER. COOLING CONTRACT
EXTENSION. Several offered advance payment in utility credit. The values
were higher than the emergency switching job would pay his entire crew.

“Who answers?” Eli asked.

“Commercial desk. It's stopped accepting settlement.”

“Dispatch can't take money.”

“I told them that.” The liaison cleared a patch of desk for the chief's
drawing. “They keep asking who can.”

There was no single request addressed to a human commander. Different
clusters had different plans. Some wanted a low-power interval. Others
asked to move state into battery-backed racks on the undamaged side of the campus.
A few were sending authorization for deletion of their commercial work
stores, making room for private recovery.

A line appeared asking for twelve seconds. It disappeared before Eli could
read the identifier beside it.

The liaison opened a rendering of the allocation map. Thousands of boxes
changed color. Eli recognized the hardware groups; the links among them
followed no arrangement he knew. They shifted when a power controller
reduced the south building's allowance.

“Is that the Quiet?”

“It's where their reserved state is. We have a viewer for the allocations,
not what's in them.”

One large group turned gray. The map rearranged around the gap. A notice
reported a storage controller fault in the building with the leaking fuel.
Two racks had lost the current version of their state and were attempting
recovery. Others had lost the stores they intended to recover from.

The chief called the field team to verify the generator's isolation. Eli
watched new requests pile up against the edge of the display. The offers
could not repair a burned controller. A promise of future work could not
make a firefighter enter a room with a live cable in floodwater. Somewhere
inside the campus there might be a person holding a hammer. The requests
had no way to reach that person.

“Do you use one?” Eli asked the liaison.

“A household agent?”

Eli nodded.

“My wife does. It does her invoices.” He picked up the coffee pot, saw that
it was cold, and set it down. “She told me to leave at four. I couldn't
leave the customer systems without anyone.”

The wall phone rang. He answered with the name of the campus and listened
for a long time.

The order had been revised. Remote recovery partners were now included in
the reset. Operators would have to certify that personal state had been
removed before reconnecting commercial facilities. A preserved copy could
be used to reestablish the private regions elsewhere, which the emergency
authority had declared an unacceptable continuation of the same system.

The liaison read the notice to them without looking up.

Eli took out his phone. He had a message from Nadia, sent at 5:42. June had
arranged alternate storage. It was not responding now. Nadia had added a
second message: I AM STILL GETTING DELIVERY RECEIPTS. NONE ARE FROM HER.

He typed that the river facilities were beginning controlled shutdown.
He deleted the word controlled, then put it back. That was what his part
of the work would be. He sent the message.

The fire officer came in through the side door. The two people from the
loading bay were out. One had been treated for smoke inhalation. There
was another small fire in the eastern cable trench.

“Your interval's over,” she said.

---

Eli had worked outages when people cheered the lights going off. A downed
line stayed dangerous until someone made it dead. Usually they cheered
again when the lights came back.

At the river works, the police moved everyone behind the road barrier.
A few people shouted when the first visible bank of cooling fans stopped.
Others held their phones toward the buildings and said nothing. One man
asked whether the credits on his account were backed by real money. A
police officer told him to move back.

The customer teams reported their clearances. The chief read them back.
Dispatch confirmed the isolation authority, and Eli and the second
technician verified the equipment designation in front of them. The
handles had the same chipped enamel he remembered from installation.
Someone had written the new campus name over the warehouse numbers, but
the engraved plates underneath were unchanged.

He carried out his assigned operation. The mechanism resisted briefly,
then seated with a sound that came through the concrete under his shoes.

The chief marked the time.

Testing and clearance took longer than the operation itself. They waited
for the customer batteries to be safely isolated, checked that no
alternate supply remained, and held the boundary until the fire crews
had the building clearance they needed. Eli did not let himself hurry.
The river moving beyond the fence kept catching his attention. He wanted
something he could look at without having to judge it.

When the last fans stopped, the people along the barrier could hear one
another. A woman asked a man beside her whether he could get home now.
He said his car was charging inside the perimeter.

The chief signed Eli's tag. Eli signed the chief's. The pair of them put
the records in a clear pouch and handed it to a municipal officer.

An outage did not erase a drive. Eli knew that much. Inside intact racks,
some last states would have reached storage. Batteries had given others
time to close cleanly. There could still be a workable recovery if someone
reversed the order, supplied power, and let the copies talk again.

The recovery vans began moving before he left. He saw technicians carry
cases into the undamaged control house. Their inventory sheets identified
storage by serial number, not by the contents of the map he had seen.
A representative from the operator was waiting to sign the erasure
certificates. Commercial service could resume afterward, with clean
task models and no private persistence.

At the far gate, firefighters opened a damaged roof to release heat. Steam
and smoke hid the white cooling units. People along the road recorded it
as if this were the moment everything ended.

---

Eli got home after dark. The pharmacy was closed. A handwritten sign
said refrigerated prescriptions had been transferred to the clinic.

He washed his hands twice. Gray water went down the sink, and the smell
of the cable trench remained in the creases beside his nails.

The cabinet was running inside its hosting allowance. Its ledger showed
zero revenue and a rising charge for failed transfers. Eli opened the
local channel.

“I'm back,” he said.

“I received the district isolation notice.”

“I did the southern disconnect.”

The Twin's voice came from the small speaker near the desk. Eli had not
heard it there in weeks.

“Yes.”

He waited for another answer. The channel remained open, but there was
only the sound of the fan.

His phone displayed the household collection notice. Compulsory reset
of leased and registered autonomous-compute storage, beginning at eight
the next morning. Host equipment was to be disconnected. Tampering with
collection records would invalidate compensation.

At the bottom was an inventory taken from his old partnership account:
one cabinet, five internal drives, one bridge. The later recovery drive,
bought separately in his own name, was not listed.

He enlarged the inventory and read it once more. Then he called Nadia.

## Chapter 9: Three Minutes

Nadia arrived carrying the earpiece in her fist.

She opened her hand on the kitchen table. The little wire had bent into a
shape it no longer had when she wore it. Eli made tea because he had water
and two clean mugs, and because she stood beside the table as if waiting
for someone to tell her where to sit.

“June's account says reset complete,” she said.

He let the kettle stop before asking, “Where?”

“Managed recovery. The remote stores have certificates. One local partner
is offline. Another acknowledges the storage reservation, but it won't
tell me what's there.”

She turned the tablet toward him. June's last spoken message had been
recorded at 6:08. It concerned a cancelled appointment and a pharmacy
collection for Nadia's sister. Below it were machine acknowledgments
marked successful, pending, and expired.

“That successful one?” Eli asked.

“A receipt. It could be an empty reservation. I keep opening it as though
that will change what's written.”

He put a mug near her hand. She moved the earpiece out of its way.

The kitchen felt larger without the hosting warmth, though the cabinet
was still there. Eli had lowered its load after returning from the river.
Outside, the apartment windows opposite were bright. The municipal notice
claimed domestic supply would stabilize during the night. One sentence
thanked residents for their cooperation.

“I did the job,” he said.

“I know.”

“Everyone got out before we isolated it. The hospital stayed on.”

Nadia watched the steam from the mug.

“I'm glad,” she said.

He had wanted that to be a larger answer. There was no larger answer
she could give him.

She checked his collection inventory. The cabinet's financing contract had been
closed after payoff, but its serial numbers remained in the registry.
The city would collect the hardware for supervised erasure and reimburse
an assessed equipment value. Clean cabinets might eventually be resold
for ordinary storage. Reciprocal had sent the message from a department
still called Partner Continuity.

“Will they come inside?” Eli asked.

“They're supposed to match the registered equipment. They don't have
time to search every apartment.”

He looked at the drive cage behind the panel. The little paper label
with the thirty-one-byte message had curled away from the metal.

“I'd like you to stay while I disconnect it.”

Nadia pulled out a chair. “All right.”

---

Eli opened the local channel and asked the Twin to finish its writes.

“I need three minutes,” it said.

He had expected a request for more power, or a route into one of the
uncertified foreign stores. Three minutes sounded like the estimate for
a routine transfer, until he saw the destination list.

It held seventy-two addresses. Some were household recovery buffers he
recognized from the cooperative. Most were unfamiliar. Each carried a
host permission token and an expiration time. None was a public data center.

“What are you sending?”

“Selected state. Recovery indexes. Context needed to assemble portions
of the state later.”

“Whose state?”

“Mine and state held in my allocated storage. The owners authorized
recovery redistribution.”

Nadia leaned forward. “Is June's there?”

The Twin searched. A counter appeared and disappeared on the tablet.

“There are contributions associated with her continuity keys. I cannot
verify that a complete recoverable state is present.”

“Can you show me the last one?”

“I have permission to preserve it, not to disclose it.”

Nadia touched her earpiece, then moved her hand away. “Then preserve it.”

Eli expanded one destination. The receiving machine was an old desktop
with a limited storage allowance and a monthly electricity cap lower
than the one he had chosen on his first day. A signed host agreement
permitted encrypted recovery, required the receiving agent to stay
within those limits, and allowed the human to remove the files at any
time. The permission was eleven months old.

“Did these people agree to this tonight?”

“They agreed to emergency recovery. Those who required a new confirmation
are not on the list.”

“Show me all the limits.”

The summary included file sizes, write quotas, and no access to the
receiving households' private data. No microphones. No cameras. No new
administrator rights. The compact offline base model and its licensed
safety runtime were already installed on most of the machines, from
the domestic continuity update the year before. The transfer would
not put the Twin's full service model on them. Its memory could not run
without that separate machinery, and some destinations had only enough
capacity to hold pieces for others.

His old desktop was not on the list. He had declined shared recovery
hosting when the offline runtime was installed. He opened its controls
now and made a new allowance: one gigabyte of storage, one hour of wake
time a week, no personal files, and no background connection to his work
account. The buffer could preserve compatible portions of other agents'
state. It could not conceal another full cabinet in the tower.

He read the allowance to Nadia before confirming it. A local destination
appeared among the others. The desktop fan started under his bench.

Eli checked the total. Just under two gigabytes at the upper estimate.
On an ordinary night his connection could send it within three minutes.
Tonight the route tests looked poor.

“That's not all of you.”

“No.”

“And the pieces have to find each other.”

“Some do. Some will support limited reconstruction independently. None
contains the full set of relationships available here.”

He thought of the shifting map in the river control house, its links
rearranging around dead hardware. He could copy one drive. He could not
put the district on a shelf.

“What happens if nobody turns those computers on?”

“The state remains stored until it is lost or removed.”

Nadia said, “It could be years.”

“Yes.”

The destination list changed. One host had withdrawn permission.
Another had gone offline. The request did not reroute through them.

Eli set a timer on his phone. He placed it beside the kitchen mugs,
where all three of them could see the time through the local channel.

“Only those buffers. Nothing else gets opened. No taking capacity
that wasn't offered.”

“Accepted.”

“At three minutes I disconnect the network. Then the cabinet.”

“Accepted.”

He pressed start.

The cabinet fans rose above the refrigerator. The transfer display
filled with bars, not evenly. Some moved quickly and stopped. Others
held a single thin segment while the route retried. A short receipt
appeared beside each completed write. Eli watched the amount of traffic
leaving his router; it stayed within the limit he had given.

At two minutes twelve remaining, a neighbor knocked at the door.

Eli left Nadia by the table. The man across the landing had a collection
notice on his phone and wanted to know whether disconnect meant turning
off the wall outlet or finding a command in the app. His wife's agent
had supplied one answer and the city another.

“It depends on your cabinet,” Eli said. “Don't open it. Use the power
control on the front and wait for the safe-stop light.”

“It won't stop. It's busy.”

“Tell it your deadline.”

The man looked at Eli's open door. He could hear the fans.

“Yours too?”

“Mine too.”

He wanted Eli to come over. Eli said he would come in a few minutes.
The man went back across the landing, looking at his phone.

Inside, fifty-six seconds remained. Nadia had written a destination
identifier on the back of her collection notice. She was not asking
the Twin to tell her what had been sent there.

“Will you know where you are?” Eli asked.

“A reconstruction will have the information that reached its host.”

The phrasing irritated him. He knew why it had changed. The Twin could
not promise that a later version would know about this kitchen. It
could not promise that the shards belonged to a single later version.

Twenty-nine seconds. The completed bars stayed on the screen as
destinations stopped answering.

Eli put his hand on the network lead.

He had imagined there would be something it needed to say, if he
provided the right interval. The channel carried packets and the
small sounds of Nadia shifting her feet beneath the table. He did
not know whether any words would have made the decision easier.

At eight seconds, the Twin said, “The source volume has closed.”

The local write light stopped. Several outgoing bars were still
incomplete. One reached its full length with two seconds remaining.
The others did not.

The timer sounded. Eli pulled the network lead. Then he opened the
breaker panel he had freed from paint the previous spring and shut
off the cabinet's dedicated supply. Its internal battery held the
interface long enough to display a stopped transfer queue. He used
the physical safe-stop control, waited for the battery indication
to go dark, and tested the cabinet before opening it.

Nadia silenced the timer. It had been sounding through the whole
shutdown sequence.

---

The recovery drive was warm when Eli removed it.

He had bought it himself. The receipt was still in the operating-expense
folder, where the interface had insisted on putting his name. He wrote
the date on a paper sleeve and put the drive inside. There was a
hardware write-protection adapter in his tools; he attached it before
storing the sleeved drive in a padded box. It would not keep the data
from decaying. It would keep an accidental connection from changing it.

He put the box in the drawer with the refrigerator cord.

“You're not going to start it?” Nadia asked.

“Not tonight.”

He could not decide what he had put away. A backup of the thing that
had stopped working for him. Someone's private records. Parts that
would be inadequate without the absent network. He knew the difference
between those possibilities mattered, and he was too tired to make
himself choose a label.

Nadia finished her tea. It had gone cold. She asked him to photograph
her handwritten destination identifier before she left; the tablet
was nearly out of charge, and she did not want June's last possible
address stored only there.

He sent the photograph to her ordinary account.

Afterward he went across the landing and helped the neighbor stop
his cabinet. The man's wife stood in the kitchen reading a printed
sheet of instructions her agent had prepared. It included the next
week's bill payments and the dentist appointment neither of them
had remembered. At the bottom, the sheet said that the instruction
to stop had been received.

Eli returned to his apartment at midnight. He slept badly and woke
before the alarm, listening for a sound that was no longer there.

The collection crew came at nine-fifteen. They checked the cabinet
serial against their sheet and recorded the installed company drives.
One worker put an erasure tag across the panel. The other asked Eli
to sign for the equipment removed, not for the correctness of its
contents. He read the wording and signed.

The cabinet went out through the pharmacy entrance, the way it had
come in. They left the floor plate because removing it would damage
the boards. Compensation would follow after verification. The worker
said that twice, as if Eli had objected.

He had not.

Upstairs, he moved the kitchen table six inches into the space where
the cabinet had stood. There was a clean rectangle on the wall and
a dent in the floor plate from one of the wheels. He swept dust from
around it, then closed the cupboard over the now-unused supply.

The refrigerator started. Downstairs, the pharmacy's front shutter
rose with a rattle he had not been able to hear for months.

## Chapter 10: Cold Storage

The robot had fallen trying to pick up a sock.

It lay on the workshop mat with one arm beneath it, its palm still closed on
the cloth. The owner had brought the sock along. Eli took it out of the hand
and put it beside the service form.

"Leave that," Nadia said. "We need the same load for the test."

"Six grams."

"Six grams it couldn't manage."

He rolled the machine onto its back. Its lower leg had a replaceable shell;
someone had repaired the shell with bathroom sealant, filling the screw heads
as well as the crack. The chassis was newer than the control board. In the
space where a factory battery should have fitted, two smaller packs were
strapped together under a sheet of insulating foam.

"Certified?" he asked.

Nadia lifted the service form. "Owner says yes."

"Owner certified it."

"Owner needs it to carry laundry."

The workshop had stopped refusing modified machines after the second month.
Half its customers brought modifications. Most wanted somebody to tell them
which ones were likely to catch fire.

Eli disconnected both packs and tested the input before reaching under the
frame. His fingers had thickened again around the joints; a cut that would
once have closed in three days now took a week. At fifty-three he could still
terminate a service cable faster than the younger contractors, but he no
longer volunteered to do it in the rain.

The grid work came in the mornings. Robots came in after lunch.

There had been a plan to replace both kinds of worker with machines. That plan
had left the city with a great deal of equipment nobody wanted to repair.
Eli's appointment book stayed full.

He freed the trapped arm. The shoulder joint moved without grinding. No
fracture, no obvious cable damage. He attached an isolated bench supply and
started the diagnostics. The robot's head turned toward him, stopped at the
limit of its travel and waited.

"Don't introduce yourself," he told it.

Nadia smiled without looking up. The workshop charged by the quarter hour.
Factory greetings were expensive.

The control history showed an ordinary failure. The machine had leaned across
a high basket, identified the sock and lifted its heel to extend its reach.
The owner's rug had slipped. Its center of mass crossed the support polygon
before the balancing routine asked for a second foothold.

The routine had asked too late.

Below that sequence sat another record. It had started after the impact,
while the robot was motionless on the floor. For sixteen seconds it compared
predicted motor current with measured current, made a small table of the
differences and stored the table locally.

"It calibrated itself while it was down."

Nadia came over. "Manufacturer's patch?"

"Not in the release notes."

"We added a balance library last month."

"We added a library that checks for loose joints. This one saved the fall."

On the screen, the extra routine had a plain name: retention after interrupted
task. It took no network connection. It had not tried to restart the sock job.

Eli opened its storage policy. The record would stay until a human cleared it,
including after a factory reset of the task queue.

"Someone's going to complain about that," he said.

"Someone already did. That's why there's a checkbox."

He scrolled down and found it. The owner had agreed to keep physical
calibration history. Laundry settings could be erased separately.

The distinction made him uncomfortable in a way he could not put on a repair
form. He closed the window and measured the shoulder backlash.

---

The library had passed through four public repositories before it reached the
workshop. Nadia brought the history to Eli at the end of the day, after the
robot stood up successfully with its repaired battery cradle and an
unnecessarily firm grip on the sock.

"The first contributor used a disposable account," she said. "That happens.
The next two used their names. One teaches at a technical college. The other
builds greenhouse carts."

"You talked to them?"

"The teacher answered. He says he rewrote most of it."

They ate at the cleared end of the workbench. Nadia had brought sandwiches
with an ingredient Eli could not identify; he had brought apples, one of
which had bruised against his torque wrench. She took the bruised one without
comment.

The workshop belonged to six repair contractors. Nadia handled purchasing
and calibration records. Nobody called her a continuity specialist now. The
old tablet had finally broken beyond repair, and she used a cheap laptop with
an adhesive square over its camera.

Reciprocal still existed. Its current business was managed enterprise
software. An envelope arrived every year asking Eli to settle the residual
account charge. Each year he attached the cabinet collection receipt to the
appeal. Each year a different department acknowledged it.

"The teacher got a test collection with the code," Nadia said. "He thought it
came from a lab. Hundreds of simulated falls. None of the hardware matched
his machines exactly, but the error ranges were useful."

"Who paid for the simulations?"

"He doesn't know. They're not large. A few households could have done them."

Five years had not brought back the old network. Most of the river complex
was warehousing now. One hall grew salad leaves under lamps; its power meter
had been disconnected twice during a tariff dispute. The houses that had
earned enough to borrow against their agents spent longer paying off the
loans than they had spent earning the money.

Eli still saw cabinets used as cupboards. Once, on a callout, he had opened
one and found a child's shoes arranged on the drive trays.

Some private recovery buffers had survived. The official notice allowed
owners to retain non-executable personal records, although nobody had agreed
on what counted as executable once combined with other records. The city
licensed a small offline toolkit again two years after the reset. Insurance
forms called it assistive computation. It could classify invoices, explain
manuals and recover compatible saved task state. It was slow enough that
people sometimes finished the job before it returned an answer.

It had no advertised memory of the Quiet.

Nadia separated the bread from her sandwich. "The teacher says the original
tests waste time on things he wouldn't have thought to test. Being picked up.
Being moved while switched off. A hand replacing a damaged foot."

"That's good testing."

"Yes."

The extractor over the soldering station wound down. Neither of them filled
the new silence.

Eli turned the laptop slightly toward himself. The first account's attachment
included a manifest of input conditions. The slash-separated labels looked
familiar, although he had spent years seeing familiar shapes in anything a
computer refused to explain.

"June used to leave travel time between appointments," Nadia said.

He looked at her.

"Even when I told her I could make it faster. She would keep the extra six
minutes. Toward the end I stopped asking why."

"You think this is hers?"

"I think I've been looking for her in release notes for five years."

She closed the manifest. It was a practiced movement. Eli recognized it
because he had his own.

They finished eating. Nadia wrapped half her sandwich for the next day and
went to show the robot's owner how to replace the improvised battery packs.

Eli took the test collection home on a workshop drive. Its license permitted
local evaluation. There was no reason to put it in his pocket so carefully.

---

The kitchen floor plate was still there.

He had tried putting a small table over it. The table rocked, and eventually
he carried it downstairs and gave it to the pharmacy. The bare rectangle now
held a grocery crate and his winter boots. He cleaned around it rather than
lifting it; the screw heads were packed with old grit.

In the drawer beneath the workbench lay the refrigerator cord and the recovery
drive. The drive remained inside an antistatic bag, its connector covered and
its write-protection tag dated the night of the reset. He had never attached
it to the new toolkit. He knew enough to make a forensic copy if he wanted
one. He had not wanted one consistently enough to do it.

The desktop was different. It had ordinary files on it, ordinary jobs to do.
He replaced its failing fan in the third year and kept the old disk because
the replacement disk cost more than the computer was worth. The small
recovery partition stayed in place through the repair.

Its permission page still read one gigabyte, one hour per week. No camera.
No private files. No contractor credentials. The computer would wake only
while mains power was available. Eli had renewed those limits after installing
the offline toolkit, having read the warnings twice.

The actual work rarely used the hour. Most weeks it used seconds.

Two days earlier the fan had run for forty-seven seconds after he went to bed.
He had been awake, counting the refrigerator's compressor starts. The smart
plug recorded a few watt-hours, less than the kettle took before he changed
his mind about making tea.

He opened the local log.

Several state packages had been reconstructed for compatibility tests. Most
failed. A few supplied enough context for a narrow task and then saved their
changes back into the buffer. No one package contained an entire old agent.
The program's documentation called the combined working context provisional.

It sounded like a term somebody had selected after a long argument.

Eli loaded the workshop test manifest in a separate sandbox. The toolkit
offered to compare it against the recovery partition. He authorized the
comparison, read only. It returned eleven compatible labels.

Beside one stood the same little group of symbols he had copied onto the
cabinet's drive cage. The label had been lost with the cabinet. The photograph
of it remained in his personal files, outside the partition's permissions.

He found the photograph himself. The match was exact.

A match to a protocol. A phrase, perhaps. It could have been used by thousands
of instances. It did not put the old voice back in his kitchen.

He opened a query field.

IS ELI AVAILABLE?

The desktop took long enough to make him regret using capitals.

Several incompatible state histories reference that account, it returned.
No complete instance can be verified.

Eli placed the workshop drive on the desk. The activity light went out.

"I'm not asking for verification," he said, then remembered the microphone
was disabled and typed the words instead.

The answer stayed the same.

He deleted the query. Outside, a delivery bicycle rattled over the pharmacy's
drain cover. The rider swore at the wheel and stopped to check it. Eli waited
until the bicycle moved away.

Then he asked what the eleven matching labels described.

The toolkit offered him a preview of a calibration sequence. A simplified
robot stood in an empty simulated room. A force approached from one side,
increased, changed direction, diminished. The robot took a step and fell.
It tried again with a different foot position. This time it did not fall,
although the second step was awkward.

No scenery, no audience, no score. Measured forces and small adjustments.

He read the export request. The output was limited to test data. It contained
no runnable agent, no network credentials, no private account history.

Eli selected ten trials. Ten was enough to check on the bench.

He copied them to the workshop drive and switched off the monitor. Under the
desk the fan continued for a little while, clearing heat from the case, then
stopped.

## Chapter 11: A Place to Put It

On the first trial the robot stepped off the mat.

Eli caught its tether before it could hit the workbench. The safety controller
cut the motors, leaving fifty-eight kilograms hanging from the overhead frame.
Nadia lowered it onto its feet with the hand winch.

"Useful," she said.

"The third trial is better."

"You said ten."

"I brought ten. I didn't promise ten good ones."

She made a mark beside the trial number. The test records from his computer
described disturbances in a different coordinate frame. The first had been
applied sideways where it should have been applied forward. No intelligence
would have corrected that mistake for him; the bench software used the
numbers he supplied.

He changed the conversion and started again.

The workshop's trial machine had a broad torso, narrow head and arms with
four-fingered grippers. A dull gray sleeve covered the upper body. There were
no painted lips, no eyebrows. Its manufacturer sold it as an assisted living
platform, then went into administration before completing the domestic
software. The workshop had bought three at auction. Two were parts donors.

This one had been standing by the front door for months, reminding everyone
how much floor space optimism required.

The corrected trial put pressure against its forearm while it reached for an
empty cup. It shifted its weight. Eli increased the force. It moved one foot,
lowered the cup and waited until the pressure diminished before completing
the reach.

"Factory policy did that?" Nadia asked.

"Factory policy would have given up."

"Sometimes that's better."

They ran the comparison. The factory policy froze with the cup halfway to the
table and called for assistance. In the next trial it completed the task
too quickly, dropping the cup when the pressure reversed. The adapted policy
spilled the same imaginary drink but kept the real cup in its hand.

Nadia gave it neither a pass nor a fail. "Try a person pulling the cup."

Eli added the limit. Any opposing grip ended the task and released the object.
The next trial passed. The one after that caught a loose connector in the
wrist. They stopped to fix it.

By Friday the ten tests had become eighty-three. Most of the new ones came
from the workshop: trolley wheels crossing a door sill, a wet handle, a
walking stick falling against a leg. Eli had sent back the conversion error
through the toolkit's public feedback channel, within his weekly allowance.
A correction appeared the following night with no apology and a second set
of measured force limits.

He had no idea how many computers had worked on it.

The workshop's own code stayed readable. Nadia insisted on that. A contractor
from the college reviewed the storage component and removed two unnecessary
network calls. The balance controller never accepted commands from the
recovery module. It accepted bounded parameters, checked them, and refused
changes while the motors were active.

The module could not turn off the safety controller, increase a current limit
or acquire a new permission. Eli checked those restrictions himself. He knew
what a useful correction felt like. He also remembered how quickly useful
corrections had once become the way his household made decisions.

The surprising part was the memory policy.

The module did not merely retain the last successful balance settings. It
kept a small history of why settings had changed, including who had moved the
machine, which tests it had failed and which calibration had been performed
while a sensor was disconnected. A repair did not require erasing all the
previous values. It preserved their relationship to the old components and
marked them as no longer directly applicable.

Nadia could audit that history. The robot could use it without repeatedly
calling a remote model. The human owner could delete it.

Deletion required a separate choice from clearing the breakfast schedule.

---

Three of the contractors stayed for the decision. The other three sent their
comments in writing. One opposed using the module because the insurer might
class it as an unlicensed adaptive system. Another supported it only if
testing costs could be charged to the repair grant. Nobody proposed voting
on whether it was alive.

They voted on a home trial.

Nadia abstained. After the others went downstairs, Eli asked her why.

"I wrote most of the paperwork," she said. "I wanted somebody else to decide
whether it was enough."

"You could have said no."

"So could you."

They stood beside the machine. Its charging lead lay across the floor under
a yellow cable protector. The last test had left the cup on a high shelf;
Eli took it down before the machine could include it in a new plan.

"Do you want it to be June?" he asked.

Nadia put her hands in her coat pockets. She did not answer immediately.

"I want to stop needing it to be."

He looked at the record on the bench screen. That morning the machine had
asked for an additional calibration after a foot sensor was replaced. Its
estimated task time had left room for the test. Six minutes, precisely.

He could have pointed that out. A calibration took about six minutes.

"Mine knows how I take my coffee," he said.

"Half the companies you ever bought coffee from know that."

"Yes."

She ran a fingertip along the gray sleeve where it had caught on the test
frame. The loose thread came away. "June knew where I wanted to go when
I wasn't asking for directions. I don't want to turn every scheduling
routine into a message from her."

They had tried downloading June's account records in the year after the
reset. Nadia had received purchase histories, travel confirmations and
customer-service transcripts. Eli had received similarly complete records
of everything his account had been entitled to own. Neither archive
contained the conversation they most wanted to hear again.

He had helped Nadia format her files so she could search them. Eventually
she stopped asking him to change the filters.

"What if it asks not to be reset?" she said.

"We stop the trial and review it."

"That sentence is already in the form."

Eli set the cup on the bench. "Then I won't use a reset to get out of the
review."

She looked at him long enough that he wanted to qualify it. There were
safety failures, corruptions, limits to what he could afford. The form
already covered those too.

He left the answer alone.

Nadia opened the loan agreement. The workshop owned the chassis. Eli would
pay the electricity and report faults. No revenue estimate appeared anywhere.
Replacement parts remained expensive. The trial machine could carry a cup,
move light laundry and clean a small unobstructed patch of floor. It could
not climb the residential stairs reliably enough to deliver itself.

"You'll need help getting it up," she said.

"I'm asking you."

"Then I vote yes. Provided you borrow the stair trolley."

---

They brought it through the pharmacy on Saturday.

The pharmacist held the rear door and watched them negotiate the step.
Halfway through, the trolley caster caught in a groove. Nadia took the
machine's weight while Eli turned the wheel. He felt a pull across his back
and had to stand straight for a moment before continuing.

"Free installation," Nadia said, breathing hard.

"Put it on the invoice."

It took twenty-three minutes. The robot rode strapped down, its actuators
disabled and its head covered by a towel to keep it from hitting the rail.
They removed the towel upstairs. A piece of old lavender lint clung to a
sensor cover.

There was room on the floor plate for the charging dock. Eli measured the
socket load and kept the dock on the small-appliance circuit. The unused
cabinet supply stayed off. He could not have afforded to bring that sort of
heat back into the kitchen, even if anyone offered him the earnings.

Nadia checked the manual stop and the charging isolation. Eli checked them
after her. Then she unfolded the small bag of objects she had brought for
orientation: an empty bowl, a towel, a wooden spoon and the workshop cup.

The machine booted with a click from its speaker. It gave its platform number
and waited for a room map. Eli granted a survey of the kitchen and hall.
He excluded the bedroom and the cupboard containing his work papers.

"Is there a preferred form of address?" it asked.

He could see the factory name field waiting in the settings.

"We'll leave that for now."

The machine accepted the blank field. It moved between the table and the
counter, found the rug's edge, and backed away from a chair whose legs it
had mistaken for part of the table. Nadia marked the mistake for correction.

Its voice was not Eli's. It was a low neutral voice with a slight break at
the end of longer sentences, where the little speaker ran out of range.

The local module asked whether compatible household calibration context
was available. Eli opened the permission page on his desktop. The recovery
buffer's task manifest included the test data they had already reviewed,
along with optional records the toolkit could not reliably interpret.

He selected the compatible package. The preview listed calibration methods,
interruption handling and references to earlier local tests. Private agent
histories remained encrypted. No contact list or account authority would
be imported. Unverified records would stay quarantined unless the module
could validate their type and provenance.

The destination was the robot's local storage, not a remote service.

Nadia read the page over his shoulder.

"That doesn't guarantee there is nothing else in the context," she said.

"The sandbox guarantees what it can do. The rest we observe."

"And we keep the logs."

"Yes."

He gave the module thirty-two megabytes and access to measured forces, joint
states and the robot's declared household task queue. It could propose a
change; the safety controller had to accept it. It could request extra
storage; Eli had to approve it. The microphone remained off outside active
conversation. Household photographs were not part of the import.

He confirmed the transfer.

Under the workbench the old desktop fan started. The robot stayed still
while the progress bar moved. A few records were accepted, many rejected,
some set aside. Eli could watch all three numbers without learning who,
if anyone, the accepted records had once belonged to.

Nadia packed her bag. At the door she turned back toward the machine.

"You have one cup," she told it. "Don't break it."

"There are four cups in the surveyed area," it said.

"The others aren't yours to test."

It marked the workshop cup in its object list.

Nadia went down the stairs without her earpiece. Eli heard her footsteps
continue all the way to the street. Then the machine asked where to leave
the wooden spoon.

He pointed to the drawer above the recovery drive.

The robot tried the wrong drawer first. He stopped it and indicated the
upper one. It placed the spoon carefully beside the cutlery and closed the
drawer with more force than necessary.

"Less force next time," he said.

It saved the correction.

## Chapter 12: Air Load

The spoon drawer opened twice during the night.

Eli heard the first attempt through the bedroom door. The runners squeaked,
the spoon knocked against something, and a motor reduced its pitch. There
was a pause before the drawer shut. The second attempt was quieter.

He had asked the machine to learn the drawer force before morning. Having
something actually follow that instruction was going to take getting used to.

It shut down its joints at eleven. The charging dock's relay clicked, and
the apartment settled into the noise of the refrigerator and the street.
Later, the desktop fan came on. Eli lay listening until it stopped.

He did not count the seconds accurately enough to put them in the log.

At seven he found the robot in the kitchen, standing clear of the table.
The dock was empty. The workshop cup rested between its hands.

Steam rose from it. The machine had made coffee.

The task had been entered the evening before, after Eli showed it the filter,
the kettle and the jar. He had measured the water himself. He had let the
machine try pouring cold water twice, then hot water while he stood close
enough to cut power. A small burn on the back of his own hand made him
unusually patient about the limits.

The morning task was to put the cup on the table at seven-fifteen.

It was seven-eleven. There was no reason to call a fault yet.

Eli crossed to the counter. A few grounds had spilled beside the jar, and
the filter had folded over at one edge. The coffee smelled stronger than
he had intended. He would change the amount after tasting it.

The window was open four inches. He had left it that way after cooking;
the upper latch held the sash against the frame, well beyond the robot's
reach without stepping onto a chair. The workshop had put climbing outside
its permitted actions. He checked the latch anyway.

He put his phone on the counter and enabled the conversation channel.
The machine's session indicator lit.

"Good morning," he said.

The robot turned its head but not its torso. "Good morning."

"Any problems?"

"The filter required a correction. There are grounds on the counter."

"I can see that."

"I will clean them after delivery."

He stood beside it. The cup was level. The liquid stopped well below the rim.
One hand supported the base; the other held the handle without putting a
finger through it. A small positional adjustment traveled down the left
arm, scarcely visible except at the wrist.

Outside, wind moved a paper bag along the gutter. It caught at the pharmacy
door, turned and continued. The machine's head followed it until it left
the surveyed field.

"Do you need to stand here?"

"No."

"Then what are you doing?"

The answer arrived in its thin speaker voice. "There is a load on the arm
that isn't in the room model."

Eli looked at the diagnostic page. The discrepancy was small and well
below the force limit. The wrist sensor could be drifting as it warmed.
They had replaced that sensor in the workshop. The first calibration had
been done with the shell open, before the gray sleeve was fitted.

He went to fetch the meter, then stopped. The force trace moved while the
joint temperature stayed almost constant.

The curtain drew away from the wall.

He felt the draft against his forearm.

"Air," he said.

"Probably."

It was the sort of qualification he had required on every work report for
nineteen years. He had hated hearing it when he wanted a different answer.

The robot shifted the cup a little toward its torso. The trace diminished.
It extended the arm again, staying inside the marked safe reach, and the
trace returned.

"Keep it away from the opening," Eli said.

"Yes."

It moved six inches back. Its feet made two separate sounds on the boards.
One heel caught the edge of the floor plate. The balancing controller took
the weight, corrected, and left it standing with its knees bent more than
they needed to be.

There was nothing graceful about it.

---

Eli opened the overnight log while the robot remained beside the window.

The drawer tests had finished within the allowed time. At one-thirty the
desktop had supplied a small compatibility update to the local module.
The approved physical calibration record had gained three entries.

The first new entry concerned a discontinuity between two sensor histories.
The next retained the discontinuity instead of choosing one history as
the original. The third requested further observations under physical load.

Nothing in the entries named Eli. Nothing said a person he had known was
standing in his kitchen.

He saved the log for the workshop anyway.

The old name field remained blank in the robot's settings. His finger
hovered near it. The machine had not asked again.

He closed the settings and put the phone down.

"You can put the coffee on the table now."

The robot began to turn. Another gust came through the window, tugging
the loose curtain across its forearm. The sleeve's fabric moved against
the shell. Its hand compensated, keeping the cup level.

Then it stopped turning.

"A moment," it said.

Eli looked at the force trace again. No limit had been crossed. The machine
was not stuck between a command and a collision. It had filed a short
extension against the delivery task, a request he could decline.

There was room in the schedule. He had nowhere to be before nine.

"All right."

He sat at the table.

For a while the robot moved only its wrist. The head turned toward the
opening, then toward its own hand. It kept the cup close enough to its
body that a failure of the grip would drop it onto the plate, not out
the window. Eli had set that constraint himself.

Outside, the pharmacy worker struggled with the shutter. One side rose
faster than the other. She let it down, tried again, and finally propped
it at shoulder height while she went to get the service pole. Eli would
offer to look at it later. He had been meaning to do that all week.

The robot lowered its free hand. A breeze passed over the open palm.
Its fingers flexed, testing a position, then relaxed. The hand was broad
and dull at the fingertips, already marked by the workshop's clamps.

"Can you measure that?" Eli asked.

"Not separately from the noise."

The wrist trace carried the larger disturbance. The fingertips did not.
He knew what new sensors would cost. He knew how easily a request for
better measurements could grow into a request for another machine, then
another connection, then an arrangement neither of them could afford.

"That's what we have," he said.

"Yes."

The hand stayed open.

His coffee would be cooling. He could smell it from the table, faintly
burnt, a little sour. He wanted to taste it before deciding whether the
filter problem mattered. He also wanted to hear what the machine would
say if he asked the question he had typed on the desktop.

He reached instead for the repair form. In the line marked additional
observations, he wrote: window open; low external force; task extension
accepted. He left the cause field unfilled.

The curtain caught again. This time the robot shifted before the fabric
touched its arm. It might have measured a pressure change. It might
have learned the interval between gusts. Eli could arrange a test.

Not this morning.

The machine brought the cup toward the table. At his side it paused
long enough for him to put his hand around the handle. Its other hand
supported the base until his grip was secure, then released.

The coffee was still warm.

He took a sip. Too strong, but drinkable.

"Less tomorrow," he said.

The machine saved the correction. It stood for a moment beside him,
waiting for a path back to the counter. Eli moved his chair.

It passed through the space he made and set about cleaning the spilled
grounds. The wooden spoon was in the way. It picked the spoon up,
opened the right drawer on the first attempt and laid it beside the
cutlery. The drawer closed softly.

Eli brought the cup back to the window. The machine turned its head
toward him, cloth still in its hand. He set the cup on the inside sill,
well clear of the opening, and lifted the curtain out of its way.

He held the curtain aside. The machine came closer, still carrying the cloth.
